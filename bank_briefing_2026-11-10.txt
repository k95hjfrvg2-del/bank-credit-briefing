Good morning. This is the Bank Credit Briefing for Tuesday the tenth of November, twenty twenty-six. Banks in Europe, the United States and Asia, through a credit lens.

Here are the headlines.

First: UniCredit posts a record quarter, raises guidance, and sets a November clock on its move for control of Commerzbank.

Second: Credit Agricole proposes to merge its Italian unit into Banco BPM, taking its stake to about forty-five percent without a bid.

Third: the biggest I P O in history is about to open its books, while Hong Kong's listing boom cools.

Behind those, today's U S inflation print, the French budget, and the money question every credit investor is asking: who is lending to the artificial intelligence build-out. After the stories you will get the Asia segment, the I P O watch, the week ahead, a cross-asset wrap, some market anecdotes, and this day in market history.

Now to the top story, UniCredit.

On Monday the Milan bank reported third-quarter net profit of about three point three billion euros. That is up roughly twenty-four percent on a year ago, and about twelve percent on the second quarter. Nine-month profit is about nine point four billion euros. Management lifted its full-year guidance to well above eleven and a half billion euros of net profit before extraordinary charges, and flagged that integration and restructuring charges will land in the fourth quarter.

Return on tangible equity ran around twenty-five and a half percent. Core equity tier one came in at about fifteen percent, and that is after deducting the buyback already approved for last year and everything accrued so far this year. Organic capital generation was about eight point two billion euros over nine months.

On distributions, the payout ratio stays at eighty percent of net profit. Roughly half goes out as ordinary dividend and thirty percent as buybacks. The interim dividend was more than doubled to about four point five billion euros, three euros a share, payable on the twenty-fifth of November. The shares rose by something between five and six percent on the day.

The credit read-across. For senior and subordinated holders, this is about as good as it gets: a fifteen percent capital ratio, twenty-five percent returns, and a management team that still has capital to spare. The risk sits elsewhere. It is the use of that capital.

Which brings us to Commerzbank. UniCredit holds roughly forty-seven and a half percent, according to press reports. It expects its remaining regulatory approvals by the end of November, and then intends to call an extraordinary general meeting to replace the shareholder side of the supervisory board, including the chairman. Earlier reports pointed to January or February, so the clock has moved forward. If approvals slip, so does the meeting.

Why does a bond investor care? Three reasons. First, consolidating Commerzbank would, on the early indications, dilute UniCredit's capital ratio at the outset, even if the combined franchise is stronger. Second, a hostile or semi-hostile control change raises the question of how far Commerzbank's own distribution plans survive. That bank has been close to completing a one point two billion euro buyback. Third, German political resistance is unresolved. The Berlin government has opposed a takeover, and nothing in this week's reporting says that has changed.

On the day Commerzbank shares rose between three and four percent. Spreads on both names barely need to move for this to be good news, since they are already tight. The asymmetry is for subordinated paper: a bigger, more diversified owner helps Commerzbank's credit, but funding-structure changes after a control shift could bring call-option surprises on legacy additional tier one. Watch for any language on that.

Second story. Credit Agricole.

The French group has proposed to Banco BPM's board that Credit Agricole Italia, its Italian subsidiary, be merged into Banco BPM. Credit Agricole holds about twenty-nine point six percent of Banco BPM today. After the merger it would hold about forty-five percent of the combined group, which would remain listed in Milan and built around Banco BPM, with the current chief executive, Giuseppe Castagna, staying on.

The terms on offer. Banco BPM shareholders would receive an exchange ratio reflecting a premium of about twenty percent, plus a one billion euro extraordinary dividend. Credit Agricole estimates about eight hundred million euros of annual synergies, and a rise of roughly twenty-four percent in Banco BPM's earnings per share in twenty twenty-seven. Completion is targeted for the second half of twenty twenty-seven.

The structure is the clever part. Under the Italian whitewash procedure, a vote of independent shareholders can waive the obligation to launch a mandatory offer. The meeting needs at least two-thirds of the capital present, with Credit Agricole and any holder above ten percent excluded from the count. So Credit Agricole crosses thirty percent, and indeed forty, without paying for control.

The credit angle. This creates the third-largest Italian bank. For Banco BPM senior and subordinated holders, a stronger and French-controlled parent is a clear plus, though the one billion euro dividend is a modest leakage from the capital base. For Credit Agricole itself, the capital cost is low, since there is no cash bid. Watch the Banco BPM board response, the position of other holders such as the foundations, and whether the Italian government again reaches for its golden power.

The sector overall: the Euro Stoxx banks index rose about three percent on Monday, with UniCredit and Banco BPM leading. Italian banks remain the sector's M and A laboratory, and the market is now pricing a pan-European consolidation premium into names far from either deal.

Next, regulators and supervisors. I found no new capital add-on, fine or consent order announced in the window from the European Central Bank, the Prudential Regulation Authority, B a Fin, or the Nordic regulators. So I will not invent one.

What did matter is the tone. The chair of the ECB's supervisory board spoke to euro-area finance ministers on Monday, and the supervisor's recent speeches have centred on simplification of banking rules and competitiveness, while preserving resilience. For holders, the direction of travel is lighter reporting burdens, not lower capital. The next hard date is the sixteenth of December, when the ECB publishes third-quarter supervisory data.

In the United States, the point is political rather than regulatory. Democrats have won a large congressional majority in last week's midterms, and press reports say the biggest banks expect more hearings and inquiries on fraud, card fees, artificial intelligence and consumer protection. The regulators themselves, the Fed, the O C C and the F D I C, still hold the pen on rules, and the Basel III endgame re-proposal from March is still waiting for a final rule, which one source puts in the fourth quarter at the earliest. Congress can make noise. It cannot easily rewrite capital rules.

Now the Europe round-up beyond the two big stories.

The Stoxx six hundred closed up about one point three percent at around six hundred and fifty-six. Banks and defence led. The French government remains the underlying credit risk in the euro area. The National Assembly rejected the twenty twenty-seven budget unanimously last Wednesday, the fourth of November, ahead of strikes. The draft aimed at a deficit of five percent of G D P next year, down from five point four, with about three hundred and forty billion euros of issuance planned.

The ten-year French yield is around four point eight percent, and the spread to Bunds is about one hundred and fifty-three basis points, one basis point tighter on the day. That is wide by any recent standard. Without an adopted budget France falls back on an emergency law to keep financing the state. For the French banks, that matters through the sovereign ceiling on ratings, through the large government bond books they hold, and through the covered bond and senior spread that follows the O A T.

Last week, B P C E reported third-quarter net income of about one point four billion euros, up twenty-five percent. That is a good result, and it fits a pattern: earnings in Europe are strong, and the sovereign is the weak link.

In the United Kingdom, the ten-year gilt is around five point one percent, a basis point or two lower on the day. Gilts continue to price a heavy fiscal burden, and U K banks' net interest income benefits from that curve while their mortgage books remain a focus.

On commercial property and Pfandbriefe, I found nothing new overnight. The key observation is the usual one: higher long yields keep pressure on office valuations in Germany and the U K, but covered bond spreads have been steady, supported by conservative loan-to-value limits.

Time for the United States.

Wall Street had a big day. The S and P five hundred rose about two point three percent and the Nasdaq about two point seven percent, with the Nasdaq one hundred up around three point four percent by one source. That is a rebound from last Friday's slump, which press reports tied to worries that the new Democratic majority could slow investment in artificial intelligence.

For the big banks, there were no earnings today. The reporting season is over. U S Bancorp reported net income of about two point two billion dollars last month with core equity tier one of ten point eight percent. The relevant points for credit today are the political risk I mentioned, and inflation.

Today at eight-thirty Eastern, one-thirty in London, the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases October consumer prices. Because of the long government shutdown, there will be no official annual rate for October. The monthly pace is expected to be moderate, with core around zero point two percent. The Fed is not pricing cuts. Market gauges put the odds of a rate hike in December at roughly three in four. A soft core number would weaken that case, and a hot one would strengthen it and push the front end and the dollar higher. Treasury yields are already elevated: the ten-year around four point eight eight percent, the two-year around four point five six, the thirty-year around five point two six.

For banks, higher-for-longer helps margins, but the same yields are the problem for unrealised securities losses and for commercial real estate refinancing.

A word on what to watch across all of this, because the three stories share a thread.

Italian and German bank credit is now being priced on who owns the bank, not only on how the bank earns. UniCredit shows what a very profitable acquirer looks like. Credit Agricole shows how a patient shareholder can build control in steps. In both cases, the senior bondholder is protected by high capital and strong earnings, while the holders of additional tier one and tier two paper are exposed to calls, restructurings and any change in the issuer's funding plan after a control change.

Practical checklist. One: read the exchange offer and the merger documents for treatment of existing subordinated debt. Two: check whether the combined entity keeps the same resolution group, because that decides where minimum requirements for own funds and eligible liabilities sit. Three: watch ratings. Agencies often put the target on review for upgrade when a stronger parent arrives, which is a spread-tightening catalyst for the smaller bank's paper before anything closes.

Also watch the supply. Banks with record profits and fifteen percent capital ratios do not need to issue much, so senior preferred and non-preferred supply from the strongest Italian names should stay light, which supports spreads through the year-end window. The heavier issuance is more likely to come from the French and the Nordics, where capital needs are rising.

Now to Asia.

Overnight, Japan led. The Nikkei rose roughly two and a half percent to around sixty thousand, recovering Friday's heavy fall of nearly six percent, as artificial intelligence and chip names bounced. The Topix gained about one point seven percent. Hong Kong was quieter: the Hang Seng closed at about twenty-four thousand four hundred and fifty on Monday and moved little on Tuesday morning. Indian banks were closed for the Laxmi Puja holiday.

I found no major Asian bank results or regulatory action in the window. The three Japanese megabanks, M U F G, S M F G and Mizuho, report on or around Friday the thirteenth of November, so that is the date to watch. M U F G was reported up more than three percent on Monday, and the story is long-term yields. Higher Japanese government bond yields lift lending spreads and bond income, but unrealised losses on bond portfolios, reported at more than one point two trillion yen at M U F G, are the sensitivity. Each twenty-five basis point Bank of Japan hike adds about one hundred and twenty billion yen to Mizuho's profits, according to the group's own presentation from May.

On the Singapore banks, D B S, O C B C and U O B have all reported their third quarter, and local commentary asks whether they are cheap enough to buy. That is an equity question, not a credit one.

One more regional point: the financing of the artificial intelligence build-out. Japanese banks are among the largest lenders to data-centre projects, and I could not confirm any specific new deal today. The point for credit investors is that this lending is now concentrated in a handful of names and a single theme, which is also why Friday's equity drop mattered to bank credit.

Time for the I P O watch.

In the United States, nothing of size priced or debuted yesterday. The story is the one that has not happened yet: Anthropic, which has filed publicly for a Nasdaq listing under the symbol A N T. The filing names twenty-two underwriters, led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley. A price range and share count are still blank in the latest filing. One report cited a range of seventy to eighty dollars a share, raising up to sixty-nine billion dollars, but another report says the company told bankers that range was inaccurate, so treat it as unconfirmed.

Valuation talk ranges from about one point one to one point three trillion dollars, against a last private valuation of nine hundred and sixty-five billion. The roadshow may start this week, and the earliest listing date being discussed is the sixteenth of November. A disclosure: the assistant that produced this episode is made by Anthropic. I am reporting the deal from public press coverage only.

Why it matters to a credit investor: fees for twenty-two banks, a test of appetite for artificial intelligence equity after a six-percent drawdown in Tokyo, and a signal for the data-centre debt that sits behind these companies. A weak book would hit more than the issuer.

Smaller deals on the calendar this week, per Renaissance Capital and subject to confirmation: vVardis, Rezera and Windward Bio. All are small biotech and similar names, and none is significant for bank credit.

In Asia, Hong Kong's boom has cooled. A South China Morning Post piece from the second of November says only three new listings have come since October, and all three closed below their offer price on debut. Two other candidates shelved their listings. Even so, the exchange is on track for one of the biggest years in its history, and more than five hundred applications are pending. A year-end rush could still come. I did not find a confirmed listing for yesterday.

Now a view, labelled as such. Banks are rallying on consolidation, not credit improvement. Equity holders are paying for M and A. Bondholders get strong earnings, but wider spreads in French debt and in high yield. With a hot inflation print and a Fed leaning to hike, the cost of holding duration in long bonds is the thing to watch.

Now the market anecdotes.

One. Japan's Nikkei is trading around sixty thousand, a level that would have sounded absurd a few years ago, and it got there on a day when it recovered a nearly six percent loss from the previous Friday. Volatility, in other words, is the price of the index.

Two. The unanimous vote against the French budget on the fourth of November. Not a narrow defeat but a unanimous rejection. In a divided assembly, every group found a reason to vote no.

Three. The Anthropic filing lists twenty-two underwriters. That is a crowded syndicate for a single deal, a sign of how much fee income the banks are chasing.

And this day in market history.

On the tenth of November, twenty twenty-one, the U S October inflation report came in at the hottest annual rate since nineteen ninety, and major stock indexes reeled. The Fed had been calling inflation transitory. Within weeks that word was dropped, and rate hikes began the following March.

The link to today: another October inflation report, another Fed debating its next move, though this time the question is whether to hike again, not whether inflation is transitory. The lesson for bond holders is that the first print after a long run of calm is often the one that resets the curve.
