Let me start with France, because it still runs through almost everything else.

One data provider put the spread between the French and German ten-year yields at one hundred and fifty point eight basis points on Tuesday, up about eight and a half basis points on the day. The French ten-year yield was about four point nine three percent, and the Bund about three point four two percent. Earlier this month the spread briefly touched one hundred and fifty basis points, a level not seen since the euro crisis years.

The trigger is political. The market doubts that the next French budget has a majority in parliament, and street unrest over the weekend added to the premium. European shares fell again on Tuesday, with the broad Stoxx six hundred index down about zero point three percent, and banks among the weakest sectors.

For credit, the transmission is the usual one. French banks hold a lot of French government paper, and their senior and subordinated spreads trade as a function of the sovereign. Analysts put the exposure of the biggest French lenders to French government debt at roughly fifteen to thirty percent of core equity tier one.

The credit default swap market tells the same story. Bloomberg reported at the start of October that five-year protection on Societe Generale's bail-in senior debt cost about one hundred and three thousand euros for ten million of notional. That was around sixteen and a half thousand euros more than similar protection on Deutsche Bank, when the two were level as recently as late August. That is a few weeks old, so treat the levels as a marker of direction, not of today's price.

The date to circle is this Friday, the twenty-third. Moody's is scheduled to review France, currently rated double-A three with a negative outlook.

One boutique research note expects a downgrade to single-A one. That is one analyst's forecast, not a decision. A prediction market gave roughly sixty-four percent odds of a Moody's or S and P downgrade by the end of the month. S and P follows on the twenty-seventh of November.

Why does a downgrade matter for banks? Rating agencies normally cap bank ratings at or near the sovereign. A one-notch move would hardly surprise anyone, and much of it is in the price. The risk is the combination: a downgrade, a failed budget vote, and a spread that has already been moving by several basis points a day. That is the sequence that pushes senior non-preferred and subordinated spreads wider, and makes issuance windows shorter.

Now, to London, and a story that sits at the crossing of three themes you follow: private credit, trading book capital and the P R A.

On Monday, the Financial Times reported that the Bank of England is examining a complaint from an insider at Barclays. The allegation is that traders booked total return swaps, referencing illiquid private credit loans, in the trading book, when the exposures should have been treated as banking book credit risk. Barclays says its roughly fifteen billion pounds of private credit exposure is treated in line with accounting and regulatory capital requirements.

The shares fell as much as three and a half percent on Monday. The size of the swap positions has not been disclosed. One blog gave a figure of about three hundred million pounds of mark-to-market exposure on one and a half billion pounds of notional, but that is a single secondary source, so I would not rely on it.

The capital logic is straightforward. A position that can be hedged and sold sits in the trading book, where the capital charge is typically lower and the market risk rules apply. A loan you cannot sell, hedged with a swap that may not offset the credit risk, belongs in the banking book. The P R A requires evidence to justify the trading book treatment.

For the credit investor, this is a conduct and capital story, not a solvency story. Barclays reports on Thursday, and the questions will be on the size of the book, the capital impact if anything were reclassified, and how many other banks have similar structures. Expect the same questions at the other large dealers with private credit financing desks. Barclays' senior and subordinated spreads should be the first place to see any contagion in sentiment.

Now to the Nordics.

Handelsbanken reports its January to September numbers at seven in the morning Central European time, so the numbers may already be out when you listen. I did not have them at the time of recording, so check your terminal. The thing to look for is the same as at its peers: credit quality, margin, and what it says about the Swedish regulator's attention to corporate risk models.

That attention is the theme. Last Wednesday, the Swedish regulator, F I, announced a temporary capital add-on for S E B because of deficiencies in its internal credit risk models. As reported, the extra core equity tier one requirement is about ten billion Swedish kronor. Risk-weighted assets rise by about one hundred and fifty billion kronor from the end of this year, and the hit to the buffer is about one hundred and twenty basis points.

The add-on stays until F I approves updated models. S E B expects to submit its applications by the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven at the latest. So, it is a long drag on distributable capital, not a one-quarter event.

S E B reports on Thursday at about half past six in the morning Central European time. The pre-close call pointed to net interest income down roughly fifty million kronor and financial income at the low end of its range. The questions are not about earnings. They are about the buffer over the requirement, the pace of buybacks, and how management will manage the extra risk-weighted assets.

Nordea showed the same pattern last Thursday. Operating profit fell four percent to about one and a half billion euros, and return on equity was fifteen point one percent. Loan losses were seventy-four million euros, or seven basis points. Core equity tier one was fifteen point eight percent, one point nine percentage points above the requirement. But the bank paused its buybacks while the E C B finishes a review of its corporate models, and the shares fell about five percent.

Swedbank reports on Thursday at seven, and Danske Bank on the twenty-ninth. Both carry large corporate books under internal models, as do D N B and the rest of the sector. For senior creditors there is no danger. For additional tier one holders, the point is the direction: when model approval is in question, the distributions are the first thing to give.

Now to Germany and Italy.

Uni Credit won E C B approval last Thursday to build its stake in Commerzbank above thirty percent, with no upper limit. After a hostile offer completed in July, Uni Credit holds about forty-eight percent. It expects to take control around the end of the year, with roughly half of the voting rights, once the remaining regulatory conditions are met. The European Commission is looking at the deal separately under merger control.

The new element since last week is political. The German government has softened its long opposition. It is now reported to be asking for conditions: keeping the headquarters in Frankfurt and protecting jobs. That is a different conversation from outright rejection, and it makes a negotiated outcome more likely.

Uni Credit shares fell about two and a half percent on Monday, to around seventy-four euros fifty-seven. Commerzbank is meanwhile buying back its own shares. It repurchased about three point one six million shares in the week of the twelfth to the sixteenth of October.

For credit, the questions are about ownership and funding. Would a controlled Commerzbank continue to issue in its own name? How would the two groups treat internal capital, and resolution planning for a subsidiary of an Italian parent? Expect the rating agencies to comment, and expect Commerzbank spreads to follow the Uni Credit parent more than the German government.

Finally, this day in market history.

The twenty-first of October nineteen eighty-seven was the day after Black Monday. The market rebounded sharply. One source puts the S and P five hundred's gain that day at about nine percent, one of the biggest single-day rebounds in October. The rebound did not end the crisis. It was followed by weeks of heavy volatility.

And go back further. On the twenty-first of October nineteen twenty-nine, there was heavy selling and panic in the American market, a week before Black Tuesday, the twenty-ninth.

The link to today is the speed. A big rebound can follow a big fall, but the first rebound is not necessarily the end. Today we have the ten-year Treasury yield, which peaked at five point three six five percent two weeks ago, and a market where one trader pays forty-four million dollars for protection against an eighteen percent fall. In nineteen twenty-nine and nineteen eighty-seven, the point is that the stress shows in funding and in credit before it shows in the equity index. That is why we watch the spreads on Friday.
