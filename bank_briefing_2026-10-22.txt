Let me start with France, because it still runs through almost everything else.

The ten-year French government bond briefly traded at five point zero zero two percent during the European session on Wednesday. It is the first time above five percent since two thousand and two. One tracker had the close at four point nine nine percent, against about three point four one percent for the ten-year Bund.

The spread to Bunds reached about one hundred and fifty-seven basis points, according to Reuters and I N G. That is close to the one hundred and fifty-nine basis point high of the second of October, and it is a level last seen in the euro crisis years. Sources differ by a few basis points depending on the time of day.

The driver is domestic. The government has a minority in parliament, and the twenty twenty-seven budget is in dispute. Press reports put the planned spending cuts anywhere between forty-three and fifty-four billion euros, and sources conflict on which is right. Street unrest over the weekend added to the premium.

Note one thing, though. Bund yields were broadly steady while French yields rose. So this is a France story, not only a global rates story.

The date that matters is tomorrow, Friday the twenty-third. Moody's is scheduled to review France, currently rated double-A three with a negative outlook. Most economists, according to press reports, expect a downgrade. That is a forecast, not a decision.

For banks, the precedent is December twenty twenty-four. After Moody's cut the sovereign, it downgraded seven French banks, including BNP Paribas and Credit Agricole. So a downgrade tomorrow would put the French bank ratings back on the table.

The mechanics are familiar. Agencies tend to cap bank ratings at or near the sovereign. French lenders hold a lot of domestic government paper. And the senior and subordinated spreads of the French banks trade as a function of the sovereign.

The market has priced much of this already. The Euro Stoxx Banks index fell about eight percent in the first ten days of October, entering a technical correction and reaching its lowest level since June. It recovered about one point four percent on Wednesday, to about two hundred and eighty-eight, but it is still down almost nine percent over the month.

My read for credit: a one-notch downgrade is probably in the price. The risk is the combination: a downgrade, a failed budget vote, and a spread that keeps moving several basis points a day. Watch the French bank senior non-preferred and Tier two spreads against the sovereign tomorrow, not the headline.

Now to the banks reporting today. Barclays first.

Barclays publishes its third-quarter results this morning, ahead of the London open. A preview put consensus profit before tax at about two point four billion pounds, against two point one billion a year ago. At the half-year, the core equity tier one ratio was fourteen point three percent, at the top of the bank's thirteen to fourteen percent target range.

What matters more than the headline is the private credit question. On Monday the Financial Times reported that the Bank of England's Prudential Regulation Authority is looking at how Barclays classifies total return swaps that reference illiquid private credit loans.

The issue is trading book versus banking book. Banking book assets generally attract higher capital. Barclays says its private credit book is about fifteen billion pounds, that the vast majority sits in the banking book, and that the conditions for trading book treatment were met. The shares fell about two point six percent on the news.

For credit investors, listen for three things on the call: the size of the trading book swaps, whether management sees any capital impact, and whether the buyback is touched. Nothing has been announced by the regulator, and the allegations are unproven.

Next, the Nordics, where supervisors are tightening on models.

S E B reports today, nine days after the Swedish regulator, F I, imposed a temporary add-on on the bank on the fourteenth of October. F I found more extensive deficiencies in S E B's internal credit risk models than previously known.

The add-on is about ten billion Swedish kronor of extra core equity tier one, on top of a separate ten billion kronor add-on in place since twenty twenty-three. It lasts until F I signs off on the updated models. S E B expects to start submitting model applications in the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven at the latest.

The wider effect, based on the figures we have been tracking, is risk-weighted assets rising by about one hundred and fifty billion kronor from the end of this year, and about one hundred and twenty basis points off the capital buffer. The shares fell about five percent on the day, to around two hundred and nineteen kronor.

The question for this morning is distributions. How much of the management buffer does the add-on consume, and does S E B keep its buyback pace? That is the same question Nordea answered last Thursday.

Nordea reported on the fifteenth. Operating profit fell four percent to one point five four billion euros, return on equity was fifteen point one percent, and loan losses were just seven basis points. But it paused share buybacks, tied to a pending European Central Bank review of its corporate risk models. The shares fell. A solid quarter, and a cautious signal.

Handelsbanken reported yesterday. Operating profit rose about ten percent from the second quarter, to about seven point four billion kronor. Return on equity was thirteen point five percent. Net credit losses were five million kronor, effectively zero. Fee income hit a record.

The shares nonetheless fell about four percent, as investors focused on margin pressure and slower loan growth. At the half-year, its core equity tier one ratio was seventeen point two percent, two and a half points above the requirement.

Swedbank also reports today. The consensus is for operating profit of about ten point four billion kronor and net credit reversals of around one hundred and twenty-six million kronor. Swedbank has not been named in the F I add-on we have seen, but the model theme runs through the whole region.

The Nordic read-across: strong earnings, tiny losses, and supervisors using model add-ons to slow distributions. For senior and subordinated holders that is positive for the loss-absorbing layers, because capital is retained. For equity holders, it is a headwind.

Now to Italy and Germany, where UniCredit and Commerzbank are moving toward the next step.

On the fifteenth of October, the European Central Bank approved UniCredit increasing its stake in Commerzbank above thirty percent. UniCredit says the remaining approvals and conditions should be finalised by the end of the year. At that point it would hold around half of the voting rights and could take control.

Yesterday, one report said Brussels opened a ten-day comment phase on the deal, with a provisional European Commission decision deadline of the sixteenth of November. Also yesterday, the German regulator BaFin fined Nomura three hundred and twenty thousand euros for late voting rights notifications. That is a fine about disclosure, not about the share purchases themselves.

For credit, three points. Commerzbank's senior and Tier two bonds would sit under a much stronger parent, which is positive, although the change of control terms matter for each instrument. The German government is still pressing for conditions. And one undated report says UniCredit's chief wants control as early as early twenty twenty-seven. Treat that as unconfirmed.

Also in Italy, a reminder of how fast consolidation is moving. A year-end overview counts about thirty billion euros of Italian bank deals, led by Monte dei Paschi's sixteen and a half billion euro purchase of Mediobanca. UniCredit's offer for Banco BPM failed after the Italian government attached conditions under its golden power. The lesson is that politics, not price, decides European bank M and A.

Finally, this day in market history.

On the twenty-second of October nineteen oh seven, the Knickerbocker Trust Company in New York opened its doors in trouble, and depositors pulled out a large sum within hours. The trust later declared insolvency. It was the start of the Panic of nineteen oh seven, the crisis that led to the creation of the Federal Reserve.

And on the same date in nineteen eighty-seven, two days after Black Monday, the F T S E one hundred fell about five point seven percent in a single session, according to a table cited by Morningstar.

The link to today: nineteen oh seven was a run on a non-bank financial intermediary that many thought safe, and the trouble spread through funding. Today we have private credit funds capping redemptions and data-centre loans that bank syndicates struggle to place. Stress shows up first in funding and in credit, then in equities.
