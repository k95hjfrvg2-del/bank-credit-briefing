Let us start with the election, because it moved everything overnight.

As votes were counted on Tuesday night, Democrats scored early wins, including a Senate seat in North Carolina. The Senate race stayed tight at the time of my sources. But prediction markets were leaning towards a Democratic sweep of Congress, and that was enough to move prices.

Standard and Poor's five hundred futures were down about point eight percent. Nasdaq futures were down about one point eight percent. The ten-year Treasury yield fell about six basis points, to roughly four point nine percent, the lowest since early September. The dollar index fell about a third of a percent, to near one hundred and one point four.

Analysts tied the move to two ideas: less fiscal stimulus ahead, and possibly tighter scrutiny of data-centre projects.

Here is the credit angle. A divided or Democratic Congress is, on the usual reading, a headwind for deregulation of large American banks, and a risk for the pace of artificial intelligence spending. Banks are lending into that spending, directly and through private credit. So an equity wobble in the data-centre trade is not only an equity story. It touches loan books, back leverage lines and securitisation pipelines.

That is my view, not a reported fact, and we have only early results. Do not trade it on this.

Now to Asia, where the reaction was sharpest.

The Nikkei fell about three point six percent by the lunch break, to around sixty thousand eight hundred and thirty-five points. That was its lowest level since the end of July. Traders sold chip and equipment names, along with SoftBank Group and fibre-optic cable makers. Reports put the full morning loss anywhere between three and a half and four and a half percent, so treat the exact figure with care.

Korea's Kospi dropped roughly three and a half percent, giving up earlier gains. Hong Kong's Hang Seng and the Shanghai Composite were close to flat. I found no bank-specific coverage for Asia this morning.

For the Japanese megabanks, the read-across is indirect, but real. They are big lenders to data-centre and technology projects, and they hold large equity portfolios. A sharp fall in Tokyo growth stocks hits their unrealised gains. A lower Treasury yield also eases the pressure on their foreign bond books, which is a small offset. Watch how the three megabanks trade today against the Topix.

The bigger Asia story for banks is in China.

On Tuesday the Financial Times reported, citing three sources, that the securities regulator held meetings with investment banks over the past few days and gave informal guidance, known as window guidance. The aim is to revive a sluggish stock market. According to the secondary reports I could read, underwriting of new initial public offerings is being suspended. Large share sales by existing shareholders are also paused until the market recovers. Other reports mention easier buybacks and a review of margin financing rules.

I could not read the original article. I am relying on relays of it, so take the detail as reported, not confirmed.

Why it matters for credit. Mainland brokers and the Hong Kong investment banking arms lose a fee stream if listings stop. Mainland banks also have indirect exposure through their securities units. And it is a policy signal: Beijing is putting equity-market stability ahead of capital raising. That can support sentiment, but it tells you how fragile confidence is.

For the Hong Kong listing pipeline, one open question is whether the guidance covers only onshore deals or reaches Hong Kong as well. The reports I saw do not say.

Now to Europe, and the biggest live corporate story in bank M and A: Commerzbank and UniCredit.

UniCredit's exchange offer for Commerzbank has now closed. Final results show shares equal to seventeen point six percent of Commerzbank's capital were tendered. UniCredit says that, added to its direct stake of twenty-six point seven seven percent, and financial instruments that give it the right to physical delivery of another three point two two percent, the total comes to forty-seven point five nine percent.

Commerzbank disputes that framing. It says the direct shares, the derivative positions and the tendered shares should not be read as one aggregate holding. It calls the acceptance level proof that the offer was unattractive, and its boards still recommend that shareholders reject it. The German government, which owns about twelve percent, has formally rejected the offer too. The transfer of tendered shares still needs regulatory approvals.

Commerzbank reports third-quarter results tomorrow, Thursday the fifth of November, at seven o'clock London time. No decision on the takeover is scheduled for that day.

The credit read-across is this. A bank that is close to half owned by an Italian group, with a German state holder and a hostile board, is a governance risk for creditors, even if control does not formally change. The key questions are the approvals timetable, whether the buyback programme continues, and what UniCredit does with dividends and capital. Senior and subordinated spreads on Commerzbank already trade tight, so there is little cushion for surprise.

Separately, supervisors have been kind to German banks. The European Central Bank cut Commerzbank's Pillar Two requirement for twenty twenty-seven by five basis points, to two point one zero percent. At least one point one eight percentage points of that must be core equity tier one. It is the second annual cut in a row. Deutsche Bank's requirement for twenty twenty-seven fell by twenty-five basis points, to two point six zero percent, while its leverage ratio requirement is unchanged.

Those announcements came in late October. They matter for the buffer to maximum distributable amount, which grows when the requirement falls.

On the United States. No major bank reported in the window. The third-quarter season ended last month, with Bank of America the stand-out disappointment on trading revenue and a regulatory penalty, and U S Bancorp posting record revenue with a net interest margin of two point eight four percent.

For American banks today, the election is the story. Large banks have been pricing a lighter regulatory touch, including easier capital rules. A Democratic House or Senate would not undo existing rule changes by itself, but it would make new ones harder, and it brings oversight hearings back. For fixed income, that tilts risk slightly towards a more cautious view on bank equity and away from the buyback story, which is a mild positive for bondholders, if it comes to that.

Next, France, because it remains the main sovereign risk for European bank credit.

The ten-year French yield is around four point nine seven percent. The spread over Bunds has been around one hundred and sixty basis points, with sources putting it between roughly one hundred and fifty-five and one hundred and sixty. The peak this year was about one hundred and seventy-six basis points on the twenty-third of October. The German ten-year yield is near three point three two or three point three three percent.

The budget is the driver. The first part, covering revenue, was due for a vote in the National Assembly on the third of November. I could not verify the outcome. There is a national strike planned for tomorrow, the fifth. Standard and Poor's reviews France on the twenty-seventh of November, and the budget deadline is the thirty-first of December. Prime Minister Lecornu's package is around fifty-four billion euros of savings.

Forecasts differ. One bank sees the spread settling between one hundred and one hundred and twenty-five basis points. Another sees it falling to around seventy-five if the budget passes largely intact. These are forecasts, not facts.

For French banks, the link is direct. They hold domestic sovereign debt, their ratings are capped near the sovereign, and a widening spread passes into their senior and covered bond funding costs. A downgrade on the twenty-seventh would be the test.

A word on commercial real estate and private credit, because the supervisory focus is shifting there.

An October report says regulators are increasing scrutiny of private credit valuations as banks and insurers hold more illiquid assets. A separate study says private credit funds and insurers hold about one point two trillion dollars of the six point four trillion dollar American commercial property market, and banks are increasingly financing those lenders through back leverage, not direct loans. Basel capital rules are pushing risk out of bank balance sheets and into this channel.

That raises the question that matters for credit investors. Bank risk has not left; it has changed form. A bank that lends to a private credit fund holds a senior claim, but one that depends on that fund's marks.

And this day in market history.

On the fourth of November nineteen hundred and three, the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at forty-two point one five. That was the low of the bear market known as the Rich Man's Panic.

And a second one, closer to today's theme. On election day in two thousand and eight, the fourth of November, American stocks rose more than three hundred points on the Dow. Over the next two days, it had its biggest two-day point decline in its history.

The lesson for today is that political outcomes do not tell you the direction of markets; what matters is leverage, and who has to sell when prices move. That is the point to watch in the data-centre trade this week.
