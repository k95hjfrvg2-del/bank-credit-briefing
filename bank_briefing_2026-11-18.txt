Let's start in Frankfurt.

The investigation is called Inside Deutsche Bank. It draws on more than fifteen million files supplied by two whistleblowers, and more than two hundred journalists from over fifty outlets worked on it, including the F T, Der Spiegel, Le Monde, the New York Times and the German broadcaster Z D F. The first wave of stories landed on Tuesday morning.

The headline allegations: the bank processed about five hundred and eighty-two million dollars of payments linked to Jeffrey Epstein that it later flagged as suspicious; it chose, as recently as twenty twenty-one, to pursue growth in Russia; and in twenty seventeen it accidentally sent a Trump tax document to another client's accountant. President Trump has called the disclosure illegal. More stories are promised this week, including on payments to a member of the Qatari royal family and on weaknesses in anti-money-laundering controls.

The shares fell as much as about six point four percent and were still down more than four percent later in the session, the biggest single drag on European banks.

The bank does not dispute that the documents are genuine. It says many incidents date back years, that it spent seven billion euros on controls between twenty nineteen and twenty twenty-five, and that it expects no significant financial impact. Chair Alexander Wynaendts backed chief executive Christian Sewing, while finance minister Lars Klingbeil said the bank must explain how it dealt with the failures.

Now the credit read. On day one, this is a reputational and conduct story, not a capital story. Deutsche Bank's core equity tier one ratio sits comfortably above requirements, and no new fine or charge has been announced. But three things are worth watching.

One: supervisory follow-up. BaFin and the E C B already have Deutsche Bank's anti-money-laundering controls on a long leash after years of remediation, including a monitor. If this week's promised stories on A M L weaknesses show problems that persisted after the remediation programme, the risk is a Pillar Two add-on, a restriction on business, or a new monitor, rather than a headline fine. That is the channel that could touch capital and distributions.

Two: U S exposure. Anything touching Epstein or sanctions has a U S enforcement angle, and U S penalties have historically been the expensive ones for Deutsche Bank.

Three: it comes days after BaFin fined the bank eight million euros over the Postbank migration. Small money, but a second reminder in a week. For senior preferred and senior non-preferred, I would expect limited spread damage unless the follow-up stories produce regulatory action. For A T one, the question is coupon and call optics, and nothing so far changes that. Watch the rest of the week.

Now to the second story: banks are getting out of data-centre loans.

According to the F T on Tuesday, I N G and Societe Generale have recently offered up to eight hundred million dollars of data-centre debt for sale. I N G's auction is about five hundred million dollars and includes loans to the operators CyrusOne, EdgeConneX and Vantage Data Centres, plus exposure to Project Jupiter in New Mexico, the same Oracle-linked project where H S B C sold a slice in the mid-eighties. Bids on the I N G block are due tomorrow, the nineteenth. Societe Generale has sought to sell about three hundred million dollars of project finance debt for Oracle-linked projects in Wisconsin and Texas.

On pricing, the reports say the Wisconsin and Texas loans were quoted above ninety cents, while the New Mexico paper was expected in the mid-eighties. And note the drift on Jupiter itself: according to earlier F T reporting, that loan traded around eighty-nine to ninety-one in September. Mid-eighties now. That is a loss of several points in two months on what was sold as infrastructure-grade risk.

Then there is the chip loan. Bloomberg reported on Tuesday that new U S rules, in force since Monday the sixteenth, have stalled a four hundred and eighty million dollar financing for Nvidia chips destined for a ByteDance-linked data centre in Malaysia. The borrower is reported to be a Singapore company, Aolani Cloud. Bankers at five lenders discussed pulling out or not joining, because a lender that funds a transaction later found to breach U S export rules could itself be exposed.

The credit read: this is banks managing concentration in A I infrastructure, not a credit event. The amounts are small against I N G's or Societe Generale's balance sheets. But secondary prices now exist, below par. Hedging is hard, because some facilities reportedly restrict credit default swaps. And there is a new legal risk layer for Asian data-centre lending. The question for the next results round: how much construction exposure, at what stage, and how is it marked.

Third story: U B S and the Swiss capital fight.

Reuters reported on Tuesday, citing three sources, that U B S is poised to accept defeat in its campaign to soften the new Swiss capital rules. The core of the dispute is the requirement to back the full value of foreign subsidiaries with core equity tier one. The upper house moved to a ninety percent target, still above what U B S holds against those units today. The total increase in requirements the bank has cited is around sixteen billion dollars.

According to the report, U B S will set out its options, including whether the tougher rules justify moving its headquarters abroad, probably with its results in early February. There is still a lower house compromise on the table that would allow more conservative valuations of foreign units, backed publicly by the shareholders Cevian and Artisan. The lower house economic committee takes it up on the twenty-third and twenty-fourth of November, ahead of the winter session. The shares fell about two percent on Tuesday.

For credit, this is the opposite of the equity story. More capital at the parent means lower buybacks and a thicker cushion beneath senior and A T one. Relocation talk is a negotiating tool for now; if it became real, it would raise resolution questions bondholders would need to price.

One more European bank item before results. And finally, this day in market history.

On the eighteenth of November, eighteen eighty-three, the railroads of the United States and Canada switched from local solar time to standard time zones. Until then, almost every town kept its own time based on the sun, which meant dozens of different times across a single railway network. At noon on that day, clocks were reset. In many places, the clock was turned back, so the town heard noon twice. It became known as the Day of Two Noons. Chicago's clocks, for example, went back nine minutes and thirty-two seconds.

What strikes me is who did it. Congress had considered national standard time and failed to act; the railroads simply agreed among themselves. The law caught up only in nineteen eighteen, with the Standard Time Act.

The link to today: in markets, the infrastructure often gets standardised by those who run it before the rule-makers arrive. That is happening in data-centre lending now: banks are setting the price of A I infrastructure debt through trades like this week's, well before supervisors have a framework for it.
