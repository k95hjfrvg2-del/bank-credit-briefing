Good morning. This is the Bank Credit Briefing for Monday the twenty-sixth of October, twenty twenty-six.

Because it is a Monday, we cover the whole weekend: European, American and Asian banks, the I P O watch, and the cross-asset picture.

Four things matter this morning.

First, the artificial intelligence credit scare. Default protection on the data-centre borrowers hit records last week, one chip-maker pulled its American I P O, and Citi is now lending to the stressed end of the sector.

Second, France. The ten-year bond is hovering around five percent, and the budget fight is far from over.

Third, supervision. Sweden's regulator has put a second capital add-on on S E B, and the European Banking Authority says capital is solid but the output floor is biting.

Fourth, the central bank week. The Fed, the E C B and the Bank of Japan all meet, with markets leaning toward a Fed hike in December.

Later: the Asia and I P O segments, a short F T review, the cross-asset wrap, anecdotes, and a date from market history.

Let us start with the story that is moving credit.

Last week the cost of insuring the big artificial intelligence spenders rose to records. CoreWeave's five-year credit default swap traded above one thousand basis points, a level analysts treat as distress. Its unsecured notes due twenty thirty-one and twenty thirty-two were yielding above fifteen percent.

Oracle's five-year protection hit a record of about three hundred and fifteen basis points on the nineteenth. Meta's reached about one hundred and eight and Nvidia's about one hundred and one, both records, according to Morgan Stanley data cited in the press.

Context: artificial intelligence related debt issuance is estimated at around four hundred and sixty-six billion dollars so far this year, against about one hundred billion over the same period last year.

Why does this belong in a bank credit briefing? Because the banks are the bridge. On Thursday, Nebius announced a three point five billion dollar secured bridge loan from Citi, signed on the seventeenth, maturing in just under a year, to fund data centres and chips. Nebius shares fell about six percent that day, even with the new financing.

That is the pattern to watch. When the bond market gets nervous, the borrower turns to a bank for a short, secured, expensive loan. The bank earns a fee and takes the risk, and often plans to refinance it into the market later. If the market stays shut, the bridge stays on the balance sheet.

The same week, S K Hynix cancelled the planned American I P O of its Solidigm storage unit, citing market volatility. Neocloud shares, including CoreWeave, Nebius and Iren, are each down by more than thirty percent this month.

The credit read-across. For the banks, direct exposure looks manageable and, so far, secured. The risk is in the underwriting pipeline and in the syndicated loans that have not yet been placed. For investors in senior bank bonds, this is a headline risk rather than a capital risk. For subordinated and A T one paper, it matters only if losses start to hit trading and fee lines together.

Now to France.

The French ten-year yield closed at about four point nine two percent last Tuesday and briefly touched five percent, the first time in more than two decades. The spread over Bunds was around one hundred and fifty-three basis points, according to figures cited from I N G.

The budget is a minority government's budget. The revenue half went to the Assembly on the twentieth. Sources disagree on what comes next: some point to a first vote on the third of November, others to a final vote on the seventeenth. The Socialists have threatened a censure motion. Treat both dates as live.

The credit read-across is direct. French banks hold large amounts of French government bonds, and their funding costs follow the sovereign. When the spread widens, it widens for B N P Paribas, Credit Agricole, Societe Generale and B P C E, usually by less than the sovereign, but in the same direction.

For covered bonds and senior preferred, the move so far is more a repricing than a panic. The risk to watch is a failed budget, a snap election, and a rating action on the sovereign, in that order.

Now to supervision and regulation.

Start with Sweden. On the fourteenth, the Swedish regulator, F I, imposed a temporary capital add-on on S E B because it found more extensive deficiencies in the bank's internal credit risk models than previously known.

The numbers, as reported by the Swedish press citing the bank: about ten billion Swedish kronor of extra core equity tier one requirement, risk exposure up by about one hundred and fifty billion kronor from the thirty-first of December, and a hit to the capital buffer of around one hundred and twenty basis points. That risk exposure figure is the bank's own estimate, as reported, not an F I figure.

The add-on stays until F I approves corrected models. S E B says it will file new applications no later than the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven. The shares fell, and one secondary report says buybacks were suspended. I could only find a single source for that, so treat it as unconfirmed.

The credit takeaway. This is a model add-on, not a solvency event. S E B still has a comfortable buffer, but the headroom to its maximum distributable amount threshold shrinks, and the distribution capacity falls. The senior bondholder is largely unaffected. The equity holder and the A T one holder pay attention to the buffer. And the read-across is to every bank using internal models, especially in the Nordics and the Netherlands.

Now Europe-wide. On the ninth, the European Banking Authority published its dashboard on the new capital rules, C R R three and C R D six. The average fully loaded core equity tier one ratio is projected at fifteen point one percent. The relative increase in minimum tier one capital from the output floor is six percent, up from five point one percent in the earlier edition. And no capital shortfalls are projected before twenty thirty, assuming unchanged balance sheets.

So the headline is comfort, the detail is drift. The floor is biting a little more with each edition.

In the United States, on the second of October the Fed published a proposal to modify the capital plan rule and the stress capital buffer. Earlier this year it decided to keep current stress buffer requirements until twenty twenty-seven. And the agencies' re-proposal of the Basel three endgame, which replaces the advanced approaches with a single expanded risk-based approach, is still in the comment phase. Direction of travel: lower or flat requirements for the big American banks, which supports distributions.

In Asia, the Reserve Bank of India issued final directions on credit valuation adjustment and on minimum capital for market risk on the seventh. That is the Indian version of the Basel market risk reforms.

In M and A, the most important live deal is UniCredit and Commerzbank. On the fifteenth, the European Central Bank approved UniCredit going above thirty percent of Commerzbank and raising the stake without an upper limit. UniCredit expects to hold about fifty percent of the voting rights around the end of the year, once remaining approvals and conditions are done.

For a credit investor, this is the one to watch. A controlling shareholder changes the debate on Commerzbank's capital distribution, its funding, and its senior and subordinated bonds. Control by an Italian parent also puts cross-border capital and liquidity questions on the table, along with the German government's view, which has been hostile.

Finally, this day in market history.

Tomorrow, the twenty-seventh of October, is the anniversary of Big Bang, in nineteen eighty-six. On that Monday the London Stock Exchange ended fixed commissions, moved from open-outcry floors to screen-based trading, and opened the door to foreign ownership of British brokers.

A year later, on the nineteenth of October nineteen eighty-seven, came Black Monday. The Dow fell twenty-two point six percent in a day, and London's FTSE one hundred fell ten point eight percent. Some commentators argue that the new, faster, cheaper trading amplified the selling.

The link to today: infrastructure changes quickly, and risk follows it. In nineteen eighty-six it was electronic trading volume. In twenty twenty-six it is the speed at which debt is raised for data centres. Both times, a structure built for good times met a bad day. The lesson is not that it ends badly, but to ask who holds the risk when liquidity goes.
