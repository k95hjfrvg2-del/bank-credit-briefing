Good morning, and welcome to the Bank Credit Briefing for Thursday the twenty-sixth of November. Large banks across Europe, the U S and Asia, read through a credit lens.

It is Thanksgiving in America. Wall Street is shut today and closes early tomorrow, so London and Asia have the stage to themselves. Here is what matters this morning.

First, the U S data dump before the holiday. Core P C E inflation came in at three point three percent, above consensus and the highest in three years. Third-quarter growth was revised up to four point three percent annualised. Jobless claims fell to one hundred and eighty-nine thousand. The market now prices a Fed hike on December the ninth as a near certainty. And yet Treasuries rallied.

Second, the Bank of England publishes its Financial Stability Report at half past ten this morning, London time. A I debt, private credit, risk transfers and gilt market plumbing are all on the list.

Third, the Bank of Korea raised rates by a quarter point to three and a quarter percent, the highest in about two years, and sharply upgraded its growth forecasts.

And fourth, oil. A Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia's largest refinery pushed crude up more than three percent, on the same day Washington floated a new round of tariffs on fourteen economies.

Later in the show: Europe, the U S and Asia, regulators and rating agencies, the I P O watch, our F T review, the cross-asset wrap, two market anecdotes, and a Thanksgiving Thursday in two thousand and nine, when Dubai asked its creditors to wait.

No watchlist bank reports results today, so let's start with the tape.

Wednesday was a day of two halves. In the morning the A I sell-off went on. The Nasdaq one hundred came close to bear market territory, and the Dow was down more than eleven hundred points at its low. By the close the Dow had turned positive, up a third of a percent at about fifty thousand one hundred and eighty-three. The S and P five hundred lost a quarter of a percent, to seven thousand one hundred and eighty-three, and the Nasdaq Composite six tenths of a percent.

The data were hot. October core P C E rose three point three percent on a year earlier, against three point two expected. Headline was three point eight, in line. G D P for the third quarter was revised up to four point three percent annualised, from three point four. Initial jobless claims fell to one hundred and eighty-nine thousand, against about two hundred thousand expected. The Fed's Beige Book was a bit softer: eight of twelve districts reported growth, down from nine, but wage pressure picked up for hard-to-fill jobs.

So inflation is sticky, growth is strong and the labour market is tight. Futures put the chance of a quarter-point hike on December the ninth at about ninety-eight percent.

Here is the twist. Treasuries rallied anyway. The ten-year yield fell to around four point eight two percent, and the two-year to around four point six. The curve steepened from the front. A seven-year auction of forty-four billion dollars cleared at four point seven one percent. The bid was a haven bid, money leaving A I equities and looking for safety, not a bet on Fed cuts.

What does it mean for bank credit? Higher policy rates support net interest income, and with growth at four percent, loan losses should stay low. But if inflation stays above three and the Fed has to hike more than once, the long end will not keep rallying, and the unrealised losses at the regionals we discussed yesterday come back into focus.

Bank shares did not join the Dow's late rally. On intraday figures, Citi was down about two and a half percent, and JPMorgan, Wells Fargo and Bank of America between one and a half and two percent. The tariff plan and the A I nerves both weighed.

Now the second story, the Bank of England. At half past ten this morning it publishes the November Financial Stability Report, together with the record of the Financial Policy Committee.

The July report flagged the Middle East conflict and the A I industry's move into external debt. Expect the committee to say more on four things. One, the debt financing of data centres, and how much of it sits with banks, either directly or through lending to private credit funds. Two, private credit itself, where redemption queues are building in the U S. Three, synthetic risk transfers, which the E C B has just put at about six hundred and thirty billion euros of protected loans in the euro area. And four, operational resilience, after nine U K bank outages since early September.

For U K bank credit, the question is not whether the banks are strong. They are. The question is whether the committee signals any change in the countercyclical buffer, or new expectations for how banks measure their exposure to non-banks. Either would feed straight into management buffers and distribution plans for twenty twenty-seven.

Third, Korea. The Bank of Korea raised its base rate by twenty-five basis points to three point two five percent, in a five to two vote. Two members wanted to hold at three. The bank raised its growth forecast for this year to three point nine percent, from three point three, and for next year to four point one percent, from two point nine. Inflation was two point eight percent in October, above target. The growth story is chips, and that is exactly the sector that crashed on Wednesday.

For K B Financial and Shinhan, a higher policy rate supports margins. The board left the door open to more, though some economists think it will lean on macroprudential tools instead, to cool household lending and property. For bank credit, that path slows growth but keeps asset quality clean.

Fourth, oil and tariffs. Yemen's Houthis said they had attacked Saudi Arabia's largest refinery. West Texas crude rose about three percent, to near eighty-five dollars, and Brent settled in the low to mid nineties, depending on the source. At the same time, the Wall Street Journal reported that the U S is moving to impose new tariffs on fourteen economies under section three hundred and one of its trade law, citing overcapacity from state subsidies.

For banks, higher oil means sticky inflation and higher rates for longer, good for margins, but it hurts energy-intensive corporates in Europe and Asia. Tariffs add trade finance risk. Neither is a capital event. Both push against the soft landing priced into tight bank spreads.

Now to regulators, supervisors and rating agencies.

The E C B's Financial Stability Review, published last week, is the backdrop for today's Bank of England report. Its message: banks are resilient, non-banks less so. Return on equity at significant euro area banks is at a ten-year high. The non-performing loan ratio fell to a record low of two point two percent in September. But provisions rose about twelve percent year on year in the first half.

On risk transfers, the E C B says the deals cut capital requirements by about forty basis points across the system, and by forty-nine basis points for the banks that use them. The ten largest issuers account for about three quarters of issuance. That concentration is the point: a handful of large banks rely on a market of non-bank investors to keep their core equity tier one ratios where they are.

In the U K, the Bank of England and the F C A last week proposed that banks plan for rebuilding their core systems from scratch after a major cyber attack.

Now the rating agencies. Moody's has been busy in Portugal and Central Europe. It upgraded Banco B P I, part of CaixaBank, by one notch to double A three, and Santander Totta, part of Santander, by one notch to single A one, both with stable outlooks. It moved the outlook on Novo Banco, rated single A one, to positive. It also moved Bank Millennium in Poland to a positive outlook at triple B one, and K and H Bank in Hungary, part of K B C, to stable from negative at single A three. S and P affirmed Eurobank in Greece at triple B minus, with a positive outlook.

Our view: the Iberian and Greek banks keep collecting upgrades while the market worries about France.

Now the Europe round-up.

European equities fell on Wednesday as the U S chip sell-off arrived in the afternoon. The Stoxx six hundred lost about one percent, to six hundred and twenty-six. The DAX fell one and a quarter percent, the CAC forty three quarters of a percent, and the F T S E one hundred a third of a percent. The ten-year Bund yield fell almost five basis points, to around three point two two percent.

France. The end-of-year finance law for twenty twenty-six was adopted on Monday, and it confirms a deficit of five point four percent of G D P, against a five percent target. The ten-year O A T yields around four point seven eight percent, and the spread over Bunds is about one hundred and fifty to one hundred and fifty-five basis points, down from the peak in late October, when the ten-year yield touched five point one three. The twenty twenty-seven budget still needs a majority, and parliament has until mid-December.

For B N P Paribas, Société Générale, Crédit Agricole and B P C E, the sovereign remains the main driver of spreads. French bank senior paper still trades wide of Nordic and Dutch peers, and with the O A T spread around one hundred and fifty, that gap will not close.

UniCredit and Commerzbank. One approval is still missing: Poland's financial supervisor, because Commerzbank owns sixty-nine percent of mBank, so control of Commerzbank means indirect control of mBank. Andrea Orcel expects it by the end of this month. On Wednesday, Italian financial press reported that the Polish approval had come through. We have not seen a statement from UniCredit or the Polish regulator, so treat it as unconfirmed.

Once Warsaw signs off, UniCredit can call an extraordinary general meeting and put its people on Commerzbank's supervisory board. Formal control or significant influence is expected in the first quarter of next year. For Commerzbank bondholders, the question we raised yesterday remains: single or multiple point of entry, and what happens to Commerzbank's own M R E L stack.

Irca: no change. U B S, Barclays, B N P Paribas and H S B C still hold about one point two billion euros of committed financing while C V C and Advent fight, and Irca's bonds trade around ninety cents.

Now to the U S.

Apart from the tape, the story to watch is private credit. In early December the large non-traded private credit funds report fourth-quarter redemption data. Tender offers at the five biggest, Blackstone's B Credit, Blue Owl Credit Income, Apollo Debt Solutions, H P S Corporate Lending and Ares Strategic Income, expire in the coming weeks.

The last round was weak. By early November, seven of the ten non-traded business development companies tracked by one monitor had prorated their latest buyback offers, meeting between thirteen and fifty percent of requests. Blackstone's flagship fund met about half.

Why it matters: the large banks lend to these funds through back-leverage and subscription lines. Redemption queues are not a bank loss, but they push funds to sell assets or draw on those lines.

Now to Asia.

After Wednesday's rout, Asia bounced. The Nikkei rose about one and three quarter percent in early trade, to around fifty-eight thousand. The Kospi rose about two percent, to around four thousand seven hundred and eighty, after a fall of more than six percent on Wednesday. The Hang Seng led, up about two and a half percent at one point. SoftBank jumped almost ten percent and the chipmaker Kioxia by double digits. With New York shut, liquidity is thin, and analysts warn that one day's rebound proves very little.

Japan. Dollar-yen trades around one hundred and fifty-nine, with hawkish Bank of Japan comments and the risk of intervention supporting the yen. For M U F G, S M B C and Mizuho, higher J G B yields mean wider margins, and all three raised their full-year profit targets earlier this month. M U F G and S M B C announced about four hundred and twenty billion yen of buybacks between them, and the three plan a joint yen stablecoin from March next year.

China. The People's Bank of China kept its loan prime rates unchanged in November for an eighteenth month, at three percent for one year and three and a half for five years. It has made the overnight reverse repo rate its main policy rate. For I C B C, C C B, Agricultural Bank and Bank of China, margins stay near record lows.

India. Bank stocks led a rally on Thursday. H D F C Bank and I C I C I Bank rose about three and a half percent each, and the Sensex gained more than three hundred and sixty points.

Time for the I P O watch.

In the U S, the calendar is empty for Thanksgiving week, after Anthropic's record listing. Anthropic priced at eighty-three dollars a share on November the eighteenth and raised about ninety-three and a half billion dollars including the overallotment, the largest I P O ever. It closed its first day at ninety-seven dollars seventeen. On Tuesday it slipped below its I P O price for the first time, to eighty-one ninety-one. On Wednesday it recovered about one percent, to eighty-two seventy-nine, still just below the offer and about fifteen percent below its first-day close.

Why it matters: the stock is now a public gauge of the A I model builders, the tenants behind tens of billions of data-centre loans.

The pipeline after the holiday. The next large deal is expected to be American Barrick Gold, a carve-out of Barrick's North American gold business, which Renaissance Capital thinks could raise about six billion dollars. Gold falling below three thousand five hundred dollars this week is not ideal timing. Also lined up is Core Specialty Insurance, about two hundred and fifty million dollars, with JPMorgan as lead underwriter.

Hong Kong. Beijing Sinotau International Pharmaceutical listed on Tuesday. It priced at fifty-two Hong Kong dollars, the bottom of its range, and opened almost six percent lower, although the international tranche was about twice covered. Earlier this month seven companies listed in one week, raising six point two billion Hong Kong dollars, almost all priced at the bottom of their ranges.

Even so, Hong Kong I P Os have raised about fifty-two billion dollars this year, according to Bloomberg, close to the twenty ten record of almost fifty-eight billion. That is a solid year of fees for the Asian franchises of H S B C, Standard Chartered, the Chinese brokers and the U S banks. In the pipeline: Alibaba's chip unit T-Head has had its listing application cleared, with a deal expected next year.

Now for our F T review: what the Financial Times is running that matters for bank credit. A note first: this morning our research tools could not reach the F T's own site, so today's selection comes from reports in other outlets that cite the paper. It is shorter than usual, and we have kept it to stories we could date.

First, the F T reported that the Trump administration has shelved its plans to use business deals with Russia as a lever to end the war in Ukraine. Why it matters for bank credit: the idea of U S companies returning to Russia had kept alive the hope that sanctions might ease. That hope fades. For Raiffeisen, and to a lesser extent UniCredit, the banks with the largest remaining Russian businesses, the base case is again a slow, costly exit, not a reopening.

Second, the F T reported on the European Union's planned fifty billion euro fund for artificial intelligence and quantum technology. The headline number is still a target, without a fixed budget. The design is that each euro of public seed money should attract up to six euros from private investors. Why it matters: Europe is trying to finance its own A I build-out, and that structure needs co-lenders. Expect the European Investment Bank, the large universal banks and the insurers to be asked to take the senior or mezzanine slices. For bank credit, that is new lending with a public first-loss layer, which is good. But it also brings more concentration in a sector where the valuations are already under strain.

Third, U S outlets, citing the F T, reported this week that Anthropic is now cash-flow positive. We mention it in one line because it bears on the A I credit debate: a model builder that generates cash is a stronger tenant for the data centres the banks are financing.

Fourth, a recall from the weekend. The Bank of England's chief economist, Huw Pill, told the F T that market pricing of rate hikes was not outrageous. For Lloyds and NatWest, higher-for-longer supports margins but tests mortgage affordability.

And fifth, worth recalling before this morning's Bank of England report: the F T's piece from last week, based on S and P research, on the risk that European banks' heavy use of significant risk transfers could force deleveraging if investors stop renewing the deals.

Taken together, the F T's coverage this week points to the same place: banks are being asked to finance the next wave, A I in Europe and capital relief through risk transfer, while the old exits, in Russia, are getting harder.

Now the cross-asset wrap.

Rates. U S ten-year around four point eight two percent, two-year around four point six, thirty-year around five point two four. Bunds around three point two two. The ten-year O A T around four point seven eight, and the spread to Bunds around one hundred and fifty. For banks: lower long yields ease the pressure on bond portfolios for now, but a hiking Fed keeps deposit costs up.

Credit. We do not have verified closing levels for the iTraxx and C D X indices this morning. The tone was risk-off in the morning and steadier at the close. Our read is that index spreads widened a little and recovered part of it.

F X. Euro-dollar is around one point zero nine five, a little softer in Asia. Dollar-yen near one hundred and fifty-nine, where the Ministry of Finance has intervened before. Sterling is supported by expected Bank of England hikes. For the Japanese megabanks, a weaker yen inflates the dollar balance sheet and the capital charge on it.

Commodities. Brent in the low to mid nineties, West Texas near eighty-five. Gold is the standout: it fell below three thousand five hundred dollars an ounce after the core P C E number, its lowest level since September of last year. On a risk-off day, that tells you hikes matter more than fear.

Our view on the day: the market is saying two things at once. The economy is too hot for the Fed to sit still, and the A I trade is too fragile to bet on. Banks sit in the middle. They benefit from higher rates and strong growth, but they finance much of the A I build-out, and that is where the losses would come from if the debt cycle turns. For now, bank spreads are pricing the first half of that story. That is our interpretation, not consensus.

The first comes from A M D. On Wednesday morning the chipmaker fell almost ten percent in the first five minutes, to about three hundred and seventy-three dollars. By the close it was down less than two percent, at four hundred and six. That is a round trip of about thirty-three dollars a share, in a stock worth hundreds of billions, in one session.

The second comes from Seoul. Wednesday's Kospi fall of more than six percent was its worst day in a long time, and Samsung Electronics and S K Hynix both lost about nine percent. A day later, the Bank of Korea raised rates and upgraded its growth outlook on the strength of chips: the industry the equity market had just dumped.

And now, this day in market history.

On the twenty-sixth of November, two thousand and nine, Thanksgiving Day in the U S, world markets woke up to a shock from the Gulf. The evening before, Dubai World, the emirate's main state holding company, had asked creditors for a standstill on its debt until at least May twenty ten. Bonds of its property arm, Nakheel, fell more than twenty points, to around eighty-seven cents on the dollar. Dubai's sovereign credit default swaps jumped from about three hundred and twenty to four hundred and forty basis points on the day, and kept widening after. Wall Street was closed, but stocks across Europe and Asia fell sharply, and European banks with Gulf exposure were the first to be sold.

There is a second date, too. On the twenty-sixth of November, nineteen ninety-seven, Japan's Tokuyo City Bank, a regional lender in Sendai, failed. It was the fourth Japanese financial institution to collapse that month, after Sanyo Securities, Hokkaido Takushoku and Yamaichi Securities. That morning the Bank of Japan reported queues of depositors outside banks.

The lesson for today? Both shocks came from a market where investors had assumed implicit state support. In Dubai, the market assumed the emirate would stand behind its companies. In Japan, it assumed that no bank would be allowed to fail. Today, the equivalent assumption is that the A I build-out will be refinanced without trouble because the tenants are the richest companies in the world. It may be right. But thin holiday markets are exactly when assumptions get tested.
