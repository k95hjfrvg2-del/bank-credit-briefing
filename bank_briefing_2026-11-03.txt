Let us start with the bond market, because it set the tone for banks yesterday.

Brent crude fell roughly eight percent on Monday, to about one hundred and four dollars a barrel. American crude fell by a similar amount, to about ninety-six dollars. It was the biggest one-day drop since the middle of May. The trigger was diplomatic. Reports said procedural hurdles in the American and Iranian talks had been cleared, with Qatar mediating, and that Tehran's leadership had backed the push.

For fixed income, the oil move matters more than the headline. Oil above one hundred dollars has been feeding inflation expectations and long yields. When it fell, longer Treasury yields eased by around four basis points, to their lowest since September, according to market reports. The ten-year Treasury sits near four point nine percent. The thirty-year is near five point three percent.

Those are very high levels. They are the reason bank securities portfolios, and commercial real estate valuations, are still under pressure, even in a quarter when earnings were good.

For banks, the read is straightforward. A lower oil price helps the inflation path, helps the case for lower policy rates later, and helps duration marks on bond books. But the Dollar index is near one hundred and two, the yen is weak near one hundred and fifty-eight to the dollar, and long yields remain close to cycle highs. One good day does not change the funding backdrop.

The second headline is artificial intelligence, and it deserves care.

On Friday, the technology sell-off was violent. On Monday, it continued. The S and P five hundred fell about half a percent, to seven thousand five hundred and forty-one. The Nasdaq composite fell just over one percent, to about twenty-five thousand one hundred and sixty. Reports say it is now in a technical correction, down more than ten percent from its October high, although sources differ on exactly which Nasdaq index has crossed that line. The Dow, helped by a rotation, actually rose about four tenths of a percent, to just under fifty-two thousand.

Why does this matter to a bank credit investor? Because the build-out of data centres is being financed by debt, and a growing share of it sits with banks, private credit funds and insurers.

Morgan Stanley has estimated that private credit could supply more than half of the roughly one and a half trillion dollars needed for global data centres through twenty twenty-eight. A Chicago Federal Reserve study from February put direct bank exposure to AI-adjacent industries at only about zero point eight percent of assets. But it flagged the indirect channel: bank lending to the private credit funds themselves.

The largest data centre lenders include JPMorgan, M U F G and S M B C, with Blue Owl, Pimco and Blackstone Credit alongside them. Moody's has made a point worth repeating. A finished data centre is not necessarily a cash-flow generating asset, and graphics chips wear out faster than loans amortise.

Here is the credit view. A sell-off in the equity of AI developers does not by itself hurt a senior secured data centre loan with a creditworthy tenant. It does hurt when the tenant is a start-up, when the collateral is chips rather than buildings, or when a bank is lending to a fund that is itself leveraged to those assets. Watch the funds, not the headline banks.

Now to the country that has been the main European stress point: France.

The spread between French and German ten-year bonds has been the headline for weeks. One market source put it at about one hundred and seventy-four basis points on the twenty-second of October, the widest since the early two-thousands. Another put it near one hundred and sixty on Monday. The two sources are not identical, so take the range. Either way, it is far wider than a year ago, and wider than Italy's spread over Germany, which would have sounded absurd not long ago.

The fundamentals are not a mystery. The finance ministry expects public debt to reach a record one hundred and nineteen point three percent of output this year, and about one hundred and twenty-two percent next year. The government's draft aims to cut the deficit to five percent of output from five point four. Economists at I N G argue the package slows the deterioration but does not reverse it. The budget has no stable majority, and there is talk of the government using a constitutional shortcut to pass it without a vote. Reports on the exact voting dates conflict, so I will not give you one. What I can say is that S and P is due to review France's rating on the twenty-seventh of November.

For bank credit, France matters in three ways. First, French banks hold French government debt in their liquidity portfolios, so wider spreads hit marks and funding costs. Second, French bank senior and subordinated paper trades off the sovereign. The big names, BNP Paribas, Credit Agricole and Societe Generale, are tied to the sovereign by rating methodology, so a downgrade would reprice that debt. Third, the European Central Bank has said it would intervene only if moves became disorderly, which tells you where the line is. We are not there.

My conclusion: this is a slow-burn problem for senior and tier two spreads in French banks, not a tail risk this week. The event risk is the rating review at the end of the month.

Now to results. The season is almost over, so here is where it ended up.

First, HSBC, which reported a week ago today and has been the weak spot. Revenue was about eighteen point six billion dollars, below the roughly nineteen billion that analysts expected. Wealth revenue was flat, which was the real disappointment. Pre-tax profit rose thirty-two percent to nine point six billion dollars, helped by the absence of a one point four billion dollar legal charge a year earlier. The expected credit loss charge was zero point eight billion dollars, about thirty-one basis points of loans. The core equity tier one ratio fell two tenths of a percentage point, to thirteen point nine percent, on higher risk-weighted assets, the buyback and lower bond values. HSBC announced a further buyback of up to one billion dollars. The shares fell about six percent.

The credit takeaway: a capital ratio under the bank's own target range while it is still buying back stock is not a credit problem at HSBC's scale. But it tells you the margin for error on distributions is thin, and that management prefers returning capital to growing it.

Second, UBS, which reported on the twenty-eighth of October. Revenue was thirteen point three five billion dollars, up five percent, with a record quarter in the investment bank. The Swiss capital debate, with the government wanting much more capital held against foreign subsidiaries, remains the swing factor for UBS subordinated debt.

Third, Erste Group, which delivered record quarterly profit above one point one billion euros, though earnings per share of two euros seventy-four were below analysts' forecasts. Central and Eastern European banks are earning well, and that is a quiet positive for the Austrian credits.

Fourth, the large American banks, which reported in mid-October. JPMorgan posted a rare earnings miss, with net income of fifteen point three billion dollars, while Goldman Sachs had record advisory revenue and Citigroup beat expectations, with services revenue up fifteen percent. The mood was shaped by AI headlines, I P O uncertainty, high fuel prices and rising borrowing costs. For credit, the message from America is that capital markets revenues are strong and consumer credit is holding. The risk is that a Nasdaq correction undermines the I P O and advisory pipeline that has driven that revenue.

I could not verify other watchlist banks reporting today. The big wave is behind us. Commerzbank reports on Thursday the fifth of November, which brings us to Germany.

UniCredit received European Central Bank approval on the fifteenth of October to take control of Commerzbank, with no cap on its stake. The remaining processes are expected to finish around year end, when UniCredit could hold about half of the voting rights, counting its derivatives. One German-language report puts it at about forty-nine point seven percent already, but that is a secondary source, so treat it with caution. Reports also mention a plan for around seven thousand job cuts at Commerzbank. The German government and Commerzbank's management have opposed the bid, though some have softened their tone as the stake approached the half-way point.

For credit investors, this is the most important European bank M and A story. A change of control can trigger rating reviews, reshape the funding structure and decide which entity issues senior preferred and non-preferred debt. If UniCredit takes control, the combined group would be rated by reference to UniCredit's strength, which is currently high. For existing Commerzbank bondholders, watch the change-of-control language and whether the group streamlines its funding vehicles. Thursday's results will show how much capital Commerzbank is carrying before it is absorbed.

Now supervisors and regulators.

The most important recent supervisory action in the region remains the Swedish regulator's decision in October on S E B. F I imposed a model add-on that raises S E B's capital requirement by about ten billion Swedish kronor. It lifts risk-weighted assets by about one hundred and fifty billion kronor from the end of this year, and it cuts the management buffer by about one hundred and twenty basis points of core equity tier one. For credit holders, it is a reminder that supervisors are using model add-ons rather than headline buffer increases. The distribution capacity of the bank falls. Senior spreads should not move much, since the bank stays well above its minimum. But tier one and the buyback pace are what take the strain.

Looking ahead on supervision: the European Central Bank published last year's supervisory review results on the eighteenth of November. This year's cycle is likely to land in the same window, though I found no confirmed date. Last year's review set the overall requirement at fifteen point six percent of risk-weighted assets, with Pillar Two at about two point one percent, stable. The latest data shows an aggregate core equity tier one ratio of sixteen percent across the big euro-area banks at the end of the second quarter. Capital is not scarce. The debate is about how it is used.

On German commercial property, the story remains one of retreat. Pbb and Aareal are both withdrawing from United States office lending, and both have looked at significant risk transfers to move loans off their balance sheets. Those moves are background and not news today, but they matter for Pfandbrief investors. Cover pools stay high quality, while the unsecured debt of the issuers carries the loss risk.

Here is my view, labelled as a view. The market is rotating out of the story it has been paying for, which is cheap energy-driven AI growth, and into the story it fears, which is expensive long money. Falling oil should help. But a falling Nasdaq and rates at five percent on the long end is a mixed message. For banks, I would favour the senior credits with strong core equity tier one and low exposure to private credit funds, and I would be careful with anything linked to French sovereign risk until the rating review passes.

Finally, this day in market history.

On the third of November, nineteen ninety-nine, the Nasdaq composite closed above three thousand for the first time. The Dow finished that day at ten thousand six hundred and nine, and the S and P five hundred at one thousand three hundred and fifty-five. At that moment, technology stocks were going straight up on the story of a new economy.

The link to today is obvious, and I will not push it too far. Then, as now, the equity story and the financing story were running ahead of cash flows. The Nasdaq peaked about four months later, and took years to recover. Today's correction is not a crash, and valuations are not the same. But what matters to credit investors is who had lent against the story. In two thousand, it was telecom and equipment lenders. This time it is data-centre lenders and private credit.
