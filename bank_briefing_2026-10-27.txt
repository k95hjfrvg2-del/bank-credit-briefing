Let us start with the story that dominated markets yesterday.

The cleaner numbers are these. Reported half-year revenue is about sixteen point three billion dollars. Cash at the thirtieth of June was one hundred and twelve point four billion dollars, plus nineteen point three billion in short-term investments. And more than eleven thousand nine hundred customers each generate over one hundred thousand dollars a year.

Why does this matter to a credit investor?

First, fees. A deal of this size, even at a fraction of a percent of proceeds, is one of the biggest fee pools in the history of equity capital markets. Goldman and Morgan Stanley are the obvious winners. Watch how the allocation of bookrunner roles is described in the fourth-quarter commentary from the banks.

Second, supply. A hundred billion dollars of new equity has to be absorbed by the same investors who own the data-centre and neocloud debt. If it crowds out other technology issuance, that could widen spreads on the weaker credits. If it goes well, it validates the whole capital structure of the sector, and eases refinancing pressure on the leveraged builders.

Third, the signal. Yesterday the Nasdaq rose and the S and P five hundred closed at about seven thousand six hundred and seventy-seven. Equity markets treated the filing as good news. Credit markets, as you know, have been more nervous about the borrowers further down the chain. That gap is the trade to watch.

That is the first story. Now to the second.

H S B C reported its third-quarter results at four o'clock this morning, London time.

Profit before tax was nine point six three billion dollars, up thirty-two percent on a year ago. Analysts had expected about nine point six nine billion, so this is a very small miss against consensus, and the headline reads as in line. Revenue was eighteen point six billion dollars.

The growth story has two parts. Net interest income was supported by higher rates. And last year's quarter carried legal provisions that did not repeat this year.

Credit quality improved. Expected credit losses fell seventeen percent to eight hundred million dollars, helped by lower charges on Hong Kong commercial property and on the wealth businesses in the U K and Mexico. That Hong Kong line matters. Hong Kong property has been the sore spot for Asian bank credit for three years, and a falling charge there is a genuine data point.

On distributions, H S B C has completed its previous buyback of about one billion dollars, finished on the twenty-eighth of September, and launched a new one of up to one billion dollars, to be completed before the full-year results. The interim dividend is ten cents a share, unchanged.

Capital. I could not find the third-quarter core equity tier one ratio in the sources available this morning. The latest number I can verify is from the half year: fourteen point one percent at the end of June, down zero point eight percentage points from the end of last year, mostly because of the privatisation of Hang Seng Bank, dividends and higher risk-weighted assets. Check the release for the updated figure.

The credit takeaway. A bank that earns nine and a half billion dollars a quarter, is cutting credit costs, and keeps buying back stock at a measured pace, is a very comfortable senior and subordinated issuer. The one thing to watch is the capital ratio, because the buyback is now running at one billion a quarter with a ratio that has already come down.

Now Europe.

The big European story this month is still UniCredit and Commerzbank. On the fifteenth of October, the E C B approved UniCredit crossing the thirty percent threshold in Commerzbank. The remaining regulatory steps are expected around the end of the year. Commerzbank's chief executive said she expects the approval very soon, and that the matter would be settled this year or early next.

Two credit angles. First, a thirty percent stake with a path to control makes Commerzbank a potential subsidiary of a larger, more diversified Italian group. That is rating-positive in principle for Commerzbank's creditors, if the funding and capital are managed well, and it raises the question of whether the structure eventually merges with Uni Credit's German arm, HypoVereinsbank. Second, Germany's government has been exploring ways to make takeovers like this harder. The politics are not finished.

One small related item. UniCredit will repay in full a one billion euro green senior bond, originally due in November twenty twenty-seven, on the fifteenth of November. It is a liability management call, and a reminder that the Italian bank has been actively cleaning up its curve.

In the Nordics, Nordea reported on the fifteenth. Now the one interpretation that ties the day together, and this is a view, not a fact. Equity markets are pricing the artificial intelligence build-out as a success story, with Anthropic's filing as the proof, while credit markets are still pricing it as a financing problem, with the French sovereign and the American long end setting the cost of money. The bridge between the two is the banks. They are the underwriters of the equity, the lenders to the builders, and the holders of the sovereign debt. A well-received Anthropic deal helps all three. A poorly received one hurts all three at once.

Now, this day in market history.

On the twenty-seventh of October nineteen ninety-seven, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell five hundred and fifty-four point two six points, or seven point one eight percent, to close at seven thousand one hundred and sixty-one. The circuit breakers adopted in nineteen eighty-eight were triggered, trading was halted, and the session ended early, at three thirty in the afternoon. The cause was the Asian financial crisis, which started in Thailand that summer, and by October had spread to Hong Kong and Korea. Frankfurt's index also fell sharply.

The link to today is direct. Korea was at the centre of that crisis, and this morning its stock market is up three point four percent on the back of an American filing. Then, as now, one region's mood moved another's, within hours. The lesson from nineteen ninety-seven is that the shock came through credit, not through equities: the banks that lent in dollars against local assets. The modern equivalent is the lending against data centres and chips, in dollars, across the world. And the market recovered the next day, which is also part of the story.
