Let's start with rates, because everything else sits on top of them.

To recap. Tuesday's October consumer price index came in hot, with core up about three tenths on the month, exactly the print Governor Waller had said would put a December hike firmly on the table. Futures now price that hike as near certain. The two-year Treasury closed Tuesday around four point seven three percent, the ten-year around four point nine eight, and the thirty-year around five point three five.

Yesterday the cash Treasury market was shut for Veterans Day, but stocks were open, and they rallied. The S and P five hundred rose about one point three percent to around seven thousand six hundred and twenty, the Nasdaq about one and a half percent, helped by chip names, and the Dow about half a percent.

So equities have voted that a hike is tolerable, while the cash bond market has not traded for a day and a half. When it reopens today, the first test is whether the ten-year holds below five percent.

The credit read-across. For U S banks, a hike into a still-growing economy is supportive of net interest income for asset-sensitive balance sheets. But a second hiking cycle reopens three questions: unrealised losses on held-to-maturity books, deposit migration toward money funds, and refinancing stress in commercial real estate. The regionals carry more of all three than the money-centre banks.

For European bank credit, the channel is mainly relative value. A five percent ten-year Treasury makes dollar investment grade very competitive for global real money, and that can drain demand from euro senior and subordinated paper at the margin. Issuers with something to print before the year-end window closes have every reason to go early.

Now to the Netherlands, and A B N AMRO.

A B N AMRO published third-quarter results yesterday, and they were strong. Net profit came in at nine hundred and forty million euros, up fifty-two percent on a year ago, against a consensus closer to seven hundred and eighty million. Total net interest income rose fifteen percent to about one point eight two billion euros. N I B C, consolidated since the first of August, contributed sixty-three million euros of net interest income in its first two months.

The bank raised its full-year guidance for commercial net interest income to above six point nine billion euros, from six point eight. Year-to-date return on equity was twelve and a half percent. The shares hit a fifty-two-week high.

Now the capital line, which matters most to us. Core equity tier one was fourteen and a half percent, down from fifteen point nine in the second quarter. The N I B C acquisition cost around seventy basis points, and N I B C brought about six point six billion euros of risk-weighted assets. Against a target of above thirteen point seven five percent, that is still a cushion of around seventy-five basis points, and the pro forma figure is fifteen point three percent.

And there is relief coming. Management expects the removal of a Dutch mortgage floor to cut risk-weighted assets by roughly seven billion euros next quarter, plus about one billion from the sale of Alfam. Both point to a rebuild of the ratio quickly.

The credit read. This is a bank that has just absorbed an acquisition and still printed strong earnings and a comfortable buffer. Senior non-preferred and tier two spreads should hold well. The one question for bondholders is how quickly the regained capital goes back out as distributions, especially with the Dutch state running a programme to cut its stake to ten and a half percent. Equity holders will want buybacks. For now, the balance looks well managed.

Now to Italy, where the consolidation story has a new chapter.

On Monday, Credit Agricole proposed merging its Italian subsidiary, Credit Agricole Italia, into Banco B P M. The terms on the table: an exchange ratio that builds in a twenty percent premium for Banco B P M shareholders, plus an extraordinary dividend of one billion euros. Credit Agricole, which already owns about twenty-nine point six percent, would end up with around forty-five percent of the combined group, without triggering a mandatory bid. The new bank would stay listed in Milan, Banco B P M's chief executive would lead it, and the board would keep a majority of independent directors.

Credit Agricole is pitching eight hundred million euros a year of synergies and a twenty-four percent lift to Banco B P M's earnings per share in twenty twenty-seven. Closing would be in the second half of twenty twenty-seven, and Banco B P M's extraordinary meeting would need a two-thirds majority of the capital represented. Banco B P M's shares jumped more than ten percent on Monday. The board has not yet given a formal public answer.

Why it matters for credit. The combined group would be Italy's third-largest bank, with a French parent at forty-five percent, deeply involved but not in full control. Banco B P M bondholders would gain implicit support from a larger, highly rated shareholder, which is positive for its senior and subordinated curve. For Credit Agricole's own spreads, the impact is modest. Watch the effect of the one-billion-euro dividend on the pro forma capital ratio, and remember that Rome has used its golden power before.

Staying with M and A. The European Commission's deadline on UniCredit's takeover of Commerzbank falls on Monday the sixteenth, and Reuters reported this week, citing people with direct knowledge, that approval is likely to be unconditional. UniCredit holds about forty-seven and a half percent of Commerzbank; Berlin about thirteen percent. The convergence of Commerzbank's curve toward UniCredit's has already done most of its work.

For Commonwealth Bank's senior and tier two, this is noise, but a useful early read on Australian mortgages.

And now, this day in market history.

On the twelfth of November, nineteen ninety-nine, President Clinton signed the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act into law. It removed the remaining barriers of the Glass-Steagall Act, from nineteen thirty-three, that kept commercial banking, investment banking and insurance apart. Citigroup, born from the merger of Citicorp and Travelers the year before, got the green light it needed. The modern universal bank in the U S starts here.

And exactly nine years later, on the twelfth of November, two thousand and eight, Treasury Secretary Hank Paulson announced that the Troubled Asset Relief Program would not, after all, buy troubled mortgage assets, as had been promised to Congress. The money would go into injecting capital into the banks instead. Markets did not like the change of course, and U S stocks fell by more than five percent that day.

The lesson for today. The first date reminds us that bank structure is a political choice; think of Rome's golden power or Berlin's board seats at Commerzbank. The second reminds us that in a crisis it is capital that restores confidence. Today's European banks hold far more of it than in two thousand and eight.
