Good morning, and welcome to the Bank Credit Briefing for Tuesday, the twentieth of October, twenty twenty-six.

This is your credit-focused read of banks in Europe, the United States and Asia, with the I P O watch and the cross-asset wrap.

Here are the headlines.

First: French sovereign stress keeps weighing on European bank credit. The ten-year spread of French government bonds over Bunds sits near one hundred and forty-two basis points, and European banks were the weakest sector in the market on Monday.

Second: in Sweden and Finland, the capital story is about models, not markets. The Swedish regulator, F I, has imposed a temporary capital add-on on S E B, and Nordea has paused its buyback while the E C B reviews its corporate risk models.

Third: Uni Credit has won E C B approval to go above thirty percent in Commerzbank, and says it expects to control the German bank by the end of the year.

After that: the bank results that still matter from last week, the Asia overnight session, the I P O watch with a debut in Seoul, a record listing in Manila and the giant Jio Platforms offer opening tomorrow, the calendar, a short review segment, the cross-asset wrap, some market anecdotes, and this day in market history.

Let me start with France, because it is the thread that runs through almost everything else.

The ten-year French spread over Germany is around one hundred and forty-two basis points. One tracker has the French ten-year yield at about four point eight five percent, against about three point four three percent for the Bund. Reuters data, carried by MarketScreener, put the spread at a two-week high of one hundred and forty-five basis points at one point on Monday.

The trigger is the budget. The government plan cuts the deficit from five point four percent of output this year to five point zero percent in twenty twenty-seven. The market does not believe the budget has a majority, and it is pricing that doubt into the spread. There is also street protest over the cost of living and public services, which adds to the political risk premium.

For banks, this matters through three channels. The first is the direct holding of sovereign bonds. Sovereign exposures were roughly thirteen percent of European bank assets at the end of twenty twenty-five, according to the coverage I have seen. The second is the funding channel, because French bank senior and subordinated spreads trade as a function of the sovereign. The third is the equity and capital channel, because lower share prices make any issuance of loss-absorbing paper harder to explain.

The numbers on the equity side are stark. The Euro Stoxx Banks index entered a correction earlier this month, roughly eight percent down at the time, and about eleven percent below its fourteenth of August peak in one report. Societe Generale is down about twenty-five percent from its August high. Credit Agricole and Deutsche Bank have each fallen more than fifteen percent from their recent highs.



Regulators argue that higher net interest income is, for now, offsetting bond losses, so this is a spread story, not a solvency story.

The date to circle is this Friday, the twenty-third. Moody's is scheduled to review France, currently rated double-A three with a negative outlook, according to a French consumer-finance article. That date comes from a single source, so treat it as likely rather than certain. S and P follows on the twenty-seventh of November, at single-A plus with a stable outlook. A downgrade by Moody's would not be a surprise to the market, but a move to the lower rungs would affect the eligibility of French collateral and the rating ceilings that agencies apply to French banks.

Now to the Nordics, where two stories sit side by side.

Story one is S E B. Last Wednesday, the fourteenth, F I announced that S E B will temporarily face a higher capital requirement because of deficiencies in its internal credit risk models for corporate and other non-household lending. F I's review this year found the shortcomings to be more extensive than the ones it identified in twenty twenty-three.

The size, as reported: roughly ten billion Swedish kronor of extra core equity tier one requirement, with risk-weighted assets rising by about one hundred and fifty billion kronor from the end of this year. That is a hit of roughly one hundred and twenty basis points to the capital buffer. One summary I saw put the increase in the capital base at about twenty billion kronor by year end, but that appears to mix in the older twenty twenty-three add-on, so I would use the ten billion figure until the regulator's own release is checked line by line.

The add-on stays in place until F I approves updated models. S E B says it expects to start submitting its applications for the internal ratings approach in the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven at the latest. So, this is a long-dated drag on distributable capital, not a one-quarter event.

S E B reports its third-quarter numbers this Thursday, the twenty-second, at about half past six in the morning Central European time. The pre-close call pointed to net interest income down roughly fifty million kronor on the quarter, financial income at the low end of its two-billion range, and stable, robust credit quality. The question for Thursday is not earnings. It is what management says about the buffer over its requirement, the pace of buybacks, and how it will manage the extra risk-weighted assets.

Story two is Nordea, which reported last Thursday, the fifteenth. Operating profit fell four percent to about one and a half billion euros, and net profit was one thousand one hundred and eighty-one million euros. Return on equity was fifteen point one percent. Net fees rose nine percent, and assets under management hit a record of five hundred and nine billion euros.

Loan losses were seventy-four million euros, or seven basis points, against a small net gain a year ago. Management kept its twenty twenty-six targets: return on equity above fifteen percent, and a cost-to-income ratio between forty-four and forty-five percent.

The headline, though, was the pause in share buybacks while the E C B finishes a review of Nordea's corporate risk models. The shares fell about five percent. Same theme as S E B, a different supervisor: when the model approval is in question, the buyback is the first thing to give.

The credit take is simple. Both banks are very strongly capitalised and highly profitable. Senior and subordinated creditors are not in any danger. But for equity holders and for those pricing additional tier one, the signal is that Nordic supervisors are tightening on internal models. The read-across is to Swedbank, which reports on Thursday as well, to Danske, to Handelsbanken and to D N B, all of which carry large corporate books under internal models.

Now to Germany and Italy.

Uni Credit won E C B approval last Thursday to build its stake in Commerzbank beyond thirty percent. After a hostile offer completed in July, Uni Credit holds about forty-eight percent. It says it expects to take control by the end of the year, with around fifty percent of voting rights, subject to remaining regulatory conditions. The German government has been clear that it opposes the deal.

For credit, the questions are about ownership and funding. Would a controlled Commerzbank still issue on its own name? How would the two groups treat internal capital and resolution planning? And what happens to the rating agencies' view of a subsidiary of a different jurisdiction? In the near term, expect rating agencies to comment, and expect Commerzbank bond spreads to track the Uni Credit parent more than the German sovereign.

More broadly, the E C B is leaning in to cross-border consolidation. Its vice president, Boris Vujcic, argued recently that Europe's gap with the United States is about capital markets rather than bank capital, since median tier one capital has more than doubled since two thousand nine to above sixteen percent. Finance ministers discussed a Commission report in September that called for less political interference in bank mergers. Scope Ratings has argued that domestic deals offer larger synergies than cross-border ones, which matters if you are weighing which mergers genuinely improve credit quality.

And one data point from the other direction. Santander agreed in February to buy Webster Financial in the United States for twelve point two billion dollars, aiming for an eighteen percent return on tangible equity there by twenty twenty-eight. Europe's banks are buying growth abroad as well as consolidating at home.

Next, the supervisors and regulators.

Two actions matter this week, and both are about internal models. The F I add-on on S E B is the larger one, at roughly one hundred and twenty basis points of buffer. The E C B review of Nordea's corporate models has stopped Nordea's buyback. In Basel Three and C R R three terms, the supervisory message is that model risk is being priced through Pillar Two add-ons and through slower distributions, ahead of the output floor phasing in. For subordinated and additional tier one holders, the lever to watch is the distance to the maximum distributable amount trigger. For senior investors, the lever is the minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities. Neither bank is close to a trigger, but the direction of travel is clear.

Now the Europe round-up.

On Monday, banks were the weakest sector in Europe, down one point four percent, Reuters reported. The C A C forty fell about one point two percent in early trade and lagged the Dax. The German market did better, which tells you the sell-off is French-specific rather than European.

On M and A, I would watch the Germany-Italy axis, where Uni Credit and Commerzbank is the live case, and the question of whether other cross-border deals follow the E C B's encouragement.

On private credit and the financing of the A I build-out: private credit funds now hold an estimated one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars of outstanding data centre debt, up from about forty billion in twenty twenty-three, according to a September analysis from Datacentres dot com. At the same time, banks remain at the table. A thirteen-bank group, with B N P Paribas, Credit Agricole, Societe Generale and Standard Chartered as coordinators, arranged about two point four five billion pounds for Virtus Data Centres at the end of September. The credit angle is the one I have raised before: banks are arrangers and warehouse lenders, while the long-term risk is migrating to non-bank holders. That is good for bank capital but changes where the next stress will show up.

Here is my view, labelled as such. The day ties together as a story about the price of duration and the price of politics. The risk-free curve is high, oil is high, and the French premium is rising. In that setting, banks with thick capital and strong earnings, such as the Nordics, are being slowed by supervisors, not by markets. Finally, this day in market history.

On the twentieth of October, nineteen eighty-seven, the day after Black Monday in New York, panic hit London. The F T S E one hundred fell more than twelve percent, its worst single-day drop on record according to Morningstar. Hong Kong had already decided to close its exchange, and kept it closed for several days.

The lesson for today is about closed markets and funding. In nineteen eighty-seven, the shock moved around the world faster than anyone could price, and the policy answer was liquidity from central banks. Today, there are Gulf tensions, high interest rates, and a sell-off in technology shares, as there was then. French bond spreads are not a crash. But the pattern is familiar: markets can look calm until a fiscal or political date forces a price, and Friday has one.
