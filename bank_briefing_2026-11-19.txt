Let's start with the deal everybody is talking about.

Anthropic priced its initial public offering late on Wednesday at eighty-three dollars a share, against a range of seventy to seventy-eight. The deal is about nine hundred and eighty million shares, and the total raise of roughly eighty-one point three billion dollars includes a private placement of about twenty-six billion. Nvidia alone is taking ten billion dollars of stock through that placement. Reports put the valuation at around one and a half trillion dollars, and the book was said to be about six times covered only three days into the roadshow.

Almost all the shares are primary. Anthropic is selling about nine hundred and fifty-six million new shares; the co-founders are selling a little under twenty-four million, and the company takes nothing from those. The stock trades on Nasdaq from today under the ticker A N T.

Now the bank angle, and there are three.

One, fees. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead a syndicate of about twenty-two banks, with J P Morgan, Citi, Bank of America, Barclays and Wells Fargo in the wider group. An Evercore I S I estimate puts the equity capital markets fee pool at around five hundred and sixty million dollars, with roughly one hundred and twelve million each for the two leads and about eighty-four million each for Bank of America, Citi and J P Morgan. Those are estimates, not disclosed figures.

Two, the lending tail. Reports on the lock-up say insiders may pledge shares as collateral for loans but may not hedge them. That is an open door for private banks and prime brokers to lend against a single, very volatile stock with no hedge behind it. Expect concentrated margin-lending books at the big wealth managers.

Three, the read-across to A I credit. A trillion-and-a-half valuation in the public market gives the whole A I build-out a fresh equity cushion, at least on paper. But it lands the same week that banks are trying to sell data-centre loans at a discount and that Nvidia shares fell after a beat. Equity is cheering; parts of the loan market are still trying to get out. Keep those two signals side by side.

The credit read for U S banks: higher-for-longer helps asset-sensitive net interest income, but a ten-year above five percent keeps unrealised losses on fixed-rate securities and mortgages large, and it raises refinancing pressure on commercial real estate borrowers. Now back to Frankfurt.

Deutsche Bank shares fell about six percent on Tuesday, their worst day in more than a year, and dropped another one point nine percent in early trading on Wednesday as the leak stories kept coming. The euro-area banks index closed down two percent at around two hundred and eighty-seven.

The new development is regulatory, but of a specific kind. The data protection commissioner of the state of Hesse has opened an investigation. It will look at whether the bank had adequate technical and organisational safeguards, and whether it told affected people quickly enough. It will not examine the content of the leaked files. Deutsche Bank says it informed the authorities itself and that no account data, login credentials or payment data were accessed.

The credit read is unchanged from yesterday, with one addition. Data protection fines on banks have historically been modest. The bigger tail risk is still the U S enforcement angle on the Epstein and Russia material, and any finding from BaFin or the E C B that anti-money-laundering weaknesses persisted after the remediation programme. Senior preferred and senior non-preferred should stay orderly. A T one is where any headline stress would show, so watch the coupon-bearing paper rather than the senior curve.

Next, Commerzbank.

According to Reuters, citing a letter from Andrea Orcel dated the eighteenth, UniCredit and the German government have settled the main disputes over the takeover. Berlin has dropped its demand for two seats on Commerzbank's supervisory board, and UniCredit has committed to add German nationals to its own board. UniCredit is also expected to propose two independent members for Commerzbank's supervisory board.

UniCredit now expects to take control in early twenty twenty-seven, with an extraordinary general meeting in the first quarter to replace the shareholder side of the supervisory board. The F T has previously reported that Orcel also intends to replace chief executive Bettina Orlopp.

For credit, the political risk premium on this deal is falling. Bondholders at Commerzbank will want to see the funding and resolution plan: whether Commerzbank stays a separate resolution entity with its own M R E L stack, or whether issuance gradually moves to the parent. For UniCredit, the questions are capital consumption, the integration bill, and how much Italian sovereign exposure the combined group carries relative to German assets. A bigger German footprint arguably diversifies UniCredit away from B T P risk, which is credit-positive over time.

Now to the supervisors.

In London, the P R A has published a consultation, C P sixteen twenty-six, on how it would use the buffer for other systemically important institutions in a crisis. The idea is that, in a system-wide stress, the P R A could temporarily cut that buffer, even to zero, and then rebuild it in stages, generally with about twelve months for each step. It would not apply to a bank-specific problem. The consultation closes on the eighteenth of February.

For A T one investors, this matters. The distance to the maximum distributable amount trigger is the key coupon-risk metric. A regulator that commits in advance to release buffers in a systemic stress effectively widens that distance when it matters most. It is a modest but real positive for U K A T one, and for the asset class's behaviour in a crisis.

In the U S, the O C C has sent the joint capital rule proposed in March with the Fed and the F D I C, which lowers some requirements, for administrative review, one step closer to final. Lower requirements mean more room for buybacks, which is equity-friendly and mildly credit-negative at the margin.

And two anti-money-laundering fines. According to MLex, Sweden's regulator, F I, fined the digital lender Northmill Bank about twenty million kronor. And H S B C's Jersey unit was fined two million pounds by the Jersey regulator, a record for that kind of case on the island.

Here is my view, and it is only a view. The market is pricing two things at once: a central bank tightening cycle on both sides of the Atlantic, and a record equity valuation for A I. Those can coexist for a while, but the transmission runs through credit. Banks are quietly reducing data-centre loans, chip-backed financings are stalling, and the biggest tech results of the quarter could not lift the stock. If Anthropic trades well today, that buys time. But the risk for bank credit is not this week's equity tape; it is the concentration of A I-linked loans, margin loans and private credit exposure in a world where money now costs five percent.

Now a couple of market anecdotes.

First, the supplier as anchor investor. Nvidia is putting ten billion dollars into Anthropic's I P O through the private placement. A chip maker buying a big slice of its own customer's listing is the purest example yet of the circular financing that critics of the A I boom keep pointing to.

Second, from Seoul. The Korean market opened late on Thursday, as it does every year on the day of the national college entrance exam, so traffic and noise do not disturb students. So the Kospi delayed its reaction to the biggest I P O in history by an hour for a university test. Some things matter more than markets.

And finally, this day in market history.

On the nineteenth of November, two thousand and eight, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell more than four hundred and twenty-seven points, about five percent, and closed below eight thousand for the first time since twenty oh three. Financials led the fall. Citigroup dropped more than twenty percent in one day, to about six dollars forty, from a peak of more than fifty-five dollars in twenty oh seven, and it fell another quarter the next day. Within days, on the twenty-third of November, the U S government stepped in with a twenty billion dollar capital injection and guarantees on more than three hundred billion dollars of Citi's assets.

The lesson for today: in twenty oh eight, senior bondholders were protected and the taxpayer took the hit. That is the regime that bail-in, M R E L and T L A C were designed to end. With the Dow now around fifty thousand, it is worth remembering that today's senior non-preferred and holding company bonds are exactly the instruments that would absorb losses next time. So when we look at stress around A I lending or at a bank under a cloud, the question is no longer whether governments will step in, but how deep into the capital structure the losses would go.
