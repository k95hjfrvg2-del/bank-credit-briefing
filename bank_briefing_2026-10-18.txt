Good morning, and welcome to the Bank Credit Briefing for Sunday the eighteenth of October, twenty twenty-six. Banks, supervisors, I P Os and markets, through a credit lens.

It is a Sunday, so no bank reports today. This is the weekend edition: the week's results, the supervisory shocks, and the week ahead.

Here are the headlines.

First, the European Central Bank has cleared UniCredit to cross thirty percent of Commerzbank and keep buying with no ceiling. Control is now a question of months, not years.

Second, the Nordic model problem. Sweden's regulator hit S E B with a fresh temporary capital add-on, and Nordea paused its buybacks while the E C B reviews its corporate models. Two of the best-capitalised banks in Europe, both reminded that model risk is capital risk.

Third, European banks have just had their worst fortnight since the spring, with the French bond market at the centre. Moody's reviews France this Friday.

Fourth, the AI financing chain cracked a little. CoreWeave's bonds now trade like distressed debt, and its credit default swaps cost around twelve hundred basis points.

Later: Bank of America's record money-laundering penalty, a quick scorecard from Wall Street, Asia, the I P O watch, the week ahead, the cross-asset wrap, and a look back at a day that matters this weekend. One note: I could not reach the Financial Times today, so there is no F T review in this episode.

Let us start with Frankfurt and Milan.

On Thursday the E C B authorised UniCredit to go above thirty percent of Commerzbank. The authorisation covers the shares from the July tender, which took UniCredit, with its direct stake and financial instruments, to about forty-seven point six percent. It also lets UniCredit keep buying in the market without an upper limit.

This is an authorisation, not a completed takeover. The tendered shares still need to transfer, and the remaining regulatory steps and offer conditions are expected around year-end, when UniCredit should hold about fifty percent of the votes. Commerzbank's board still calls the offer financially inadequate. Berlin wants guarantees on the Frankfurt headquarters, jobs and lending to mid-sized companies. Commerzbank reports third-quarter results on the fifth of November, and the European competition verdict is reportedly due around the sixteenth of November.

The credit read-across. For Commerzbank senior and subordinated holders, the end-state is a subsidiary of an investment-grade Italian parent with a stronger capital machine, which supports spreads. The risk is in the middle. A majority holder that wants capital back may squeeze distributions, rework the funding structure, and decide how much of Commerzbank's debt stack is worth keeping outstanding. Watch call decisions on Commerzbank additional tier one and Tier two paper, and any change in how rating agencies treat group support. For UniCredit paper, execution risk and the cost of buying control are the questions, not solvency.

Story two. The supervisors have turned their attention to internal models, and it is hitting Nordic banks.

On Wednesday the Swedish regulator, F I, said it found more extensive deficiencies in S E B's internal credit risk models than it knew about before. It imposed a temporary capital add-on on top of the one S E B has carried since twenty twenty-three. Press reports put the extra core equity tier one requirement at about ten billion Swedish kronor. The aim is to cover the shortfall until the models are fixed.

Then on Thursday, Nordea reported third-quarter operating profit of one thousand five hundred and forty-one million euros, about two percent under the Bloomberg consensus. The return on equity was fifteen point one percent. The headline was the buyback. Nordea paused repurchases until it knows what the E C B review of its corporate lending models will do to risk-weighted assets. Management said the latest discussions point to more caution in the models, and does not expect decisions before twenty twenty-seven. The shares fell about five percent. Core equity tier one stood at fifteen point eight percent, one point nine percentage points above the requirement.

Now the credit view. Neither bank has a solvency problem. Nordea's buffer is large, and S E B remains comfortably capitalised. But the pattern matters. Supervisors are using model reviews and add-ons to pull capital up, and they are doing it on the banks we thought were the safest. For senior and senior non-preferred holders it is mildly positive: capital is going up, and distributions are going down. For equity investors it is a headwind. For additional tier one, the distance to the maximum distributable amount trigger shrinks when requirements rise, so keep an eye on S E B's headroom when it reports on Thursday.

Story three. European banks have had a rough fortnight.

The Euro Stoxx banks index fell about eight percent over two weeks to the tenth of October, entering a technical correction. Societe Generale and Deutsche Bank dropped more than five percent on a single day, the seventh. The trigger was French politics and higher bond yields. At the start of the month the gap between French and German ten-year yields briefly reached about one hundred and fifty basis points, a level last seen in the euro crisis years. A tracker put it at about one hundred and thirty-seven basis points on Friday, with the French ten-year yield at four point seven nine percent and the Bund at three point four two percent. That is one source, so treat the decimals with care.

The calendar matters. Prime Minister Lecornu's twenty twenty-seven budget aims for a deficit of five percent of gross domestic product, down from five point four. Fitch warned on the twelfth that even five percent does not stabilise the debt, which it puts at roughly three percent. Moody's, at A a three with a negative outlook, is scheduled to review France on Friday, the twenty-third of October. Some sources say the twenty-fourth. S and P follows on the twenty-seventh of November.

For bank credit, France matters in three ways. French banks hold large sovereign books and are the first to feel a wider OAT spread. Their funding costs are tied to the sovereign ceiling. And covered bonds and senior paper from French issuers trade with the country. One data point I would flag: French names inside the iTraxx Main index still trade inside the index, even while French sovereign credit default swaps set records. That divergence will not last forever. Either the banks widen or the sovereign calms.

Story four. The AI financing chain.

CoreWeave, which has about fifty billion dollars of debt according to L S E G, saw its bonds fall to distressed levels this week. Notes due twenty thirty-one and twenty thirty-two traded between about seventy-six and eighty-one cents on the dollar, yielding above fifteen percent. Five-year credit default swaps reached about twelve hundred basis points. The shares fell roughly sixteen percent on Wednesday, and chief executive Mike Intrator called the reaction irrational. They recovered after Nvidia's Jensen Huang said CoreWeave's financing costs are falling.

Why this matters to a bank credit investor. Private credit and the banks behind it are financing a build-out that Carlyle thinks could need about one trillion dollars from private lenders alone. An S M B C strategist told Reuters investors still want these deals but demand more compensation, especially for projects far from completion. The question for European and Japanese banks is not the single name. It is the back-leverage, the warehouse lines and the significant risk transfers that sit behind the data-centre boom. I could not find reporting that names specific bank exposures to CoreWeave, so I will not guess. But when the spread on one of the largest borrowers in the chain goes from tight to twelve hundred in a week, loan marks and covenant tests deserve a second look.

Now, the week's results, briefly. Nothing reports today, but four large banks' third-quarter numbers shape the tone.

Bank of America. Profit fell seventeen percent to six point nine three billion dollars, or ninety-one cents a share. The cause was a one point two billion dollar penalty, announced the day before, for failing over several years to report more than five billion dollars of suspicious transactions. The Comptroller of the Currency's part was a record seven hundred and fifty million dollars. The charge is not tax deductible and cost seventeen cents a share. Without it, earnings would have been about one dollar and eight cents, a little under the consensus of one dollar ten. Credit takeaway: a large fine, a clean balance sheet. The penalty is a one-off in capital terms and does not change the credit story. The reputational and supervisory follow-up is what to watch, because consent orders can limit growth.

JPMorgan. Net income was fifteen point three billion dollars on revenue of fifty point nine billion. Earnings per share of five dollars fifty-seven missed an estimate of five ninety-one, partly because of a six hundred and fifty-eight million dollar securities loss. Credit costs were two point seven billion dollars, including two point two billion of net charge-offs and a five hundred and thirty-nine million dollar reserve build, which the bank put down to new wholesale lending rather than worse borrowers. It raised its twenty twenty-six net interest income outlook to about one hundred and six point two billion dollars. Press-summary numbers, so check the release.

Goldman Sachs. Equities trading revenue rose seventy-two percent to about six point four billion dollars, and diluted earnings per share were seventeen dollars and one cent, well above expectations, after a four hundred and fifty million dollar charitable contribution. Morgan Stanley's equities desk made about five point eight billion, up forty-one percent. Trading is carrying the sector.

Citigroup. Revenue of twenty-three point eight billion dollars and earnings of two dollars eighty-five beat forecasts. Core equity tier one was twelve point eight percent, about one hundred and twenty basis points above its requirement, and it bought back three billion dollars of stock. The return on tangible equity was eleven point five percent.

Wells Fargo earned five point nine five billion dollars, up six and a half percent, with a billion dollars of provisions and net charge-offs of nine hundred and eighty-three million. U S Bancorp reported record revenue of eight billion dollars and M and T earned seven hundred and seventy-five million. The credit message from the US is steady consumers and wholesale books, supported by trading, not by lending.

Regulators and supervisors. Beyond S E B, Nordea and Bank of America, the E C B stressed on the fifteenth that any rule changes must keep the stronger capital base European banks have built since twenty twelve, with a Supervisory Board member saying uncertainty is rising. That follows a push to ease requirements for small, simple banks. Separately, from the first of October the E C B lets banks put material credit-risk model changes into use soon after filing, but with a capital floor on changes that lower risk weights. That is the other side of the Nordic story: faster approvals, tougher floors. And at the Bank of England, the Prudential Regulation Authority's Basel three point one timetable points to January twenty twenty-seven, though I found no new PRA announcement this week.

Europe round-up. Beyond Frankfurt and Stockholm, the story is rates. The European Central Bank has raised its deposit rate to two point five percent and markets give about seventy-seven percent odds of a hike to three percent in December. It meets on the twenty-ninth, and a hold is expected. The Bank of England holds bank rate at three point seven five percent after a six-three vote in September, with three members voting to hike. The ten-year gilt yield rose to five point three eight percent on Friday, and the thirty-year touched six percent on the first of October, the first time since nineteen ninety-eight. Higher rates lift net interest income for UK banks but strain mortgage affordability and commercial property valuations. For Pfandbrief and commercial real estate lenders, higher yields mean higher refinancing hurdles, and that deserves attention going into year-end.

Nothing else material moved in the large Southern European or UK banks over the weekend. I will leave it at that rather than pad.

US round-up. Beyond results, the story is that the Federal Reserve hiked by twenty-five basis points in September, to three point seven five to four percent. Officials say there is no need to move at consecutive meetings, and the next one is on the twenty-eighth of October, with a hold expected. Wall Street's banks report a strong deal pipeline despite volatility. Jeremy Barnum at JPMorgan said deals are more delayed than cancelled, and Goldman's David Solomon said he does not see clients sitting on the sidelines because of the midterms. Regional banks continue: Fifth Third reports tomorrow before the open, along with several smaller names.

Now to Asia.

There are no large Asian bank results this weekend. The point that matters for Asian banks is yields. The Japanese ten-year government bond yield hit about three point one percent in early October, the highest since twenty twenty-two, and the thirty-year was at about four percent on Friday. For the Japanese megabanks that means wider margins and rising unrealised bond losses at the same time, with M U F G next reporting on the thirteenth of November.

In China, the latest data are from the second quarter. The industry net interest margin was one point four one percent, its first quarterly rise since twenty twenty-two, and the large banks' non-performing loan ratio was one point two one percent. Third-quarter results from the big five come in late October. Hong Kong markets are closed tomorrow for a holiday and reopen on Tuesday.

It priced at the bottom of its range, and the shares traded about four percent below the offer price by midday. Now the cross-asset wrap, using Friday's closes.

Rates. The US ten-year yield closed near five point one percent, after its largest weekly fall since February, down about fifteen basis points. The two-year was near four point seven three percent. So what for banks: the curve is flat to mildly inverted, which does not help net interest income growth for the American lenders, but a weekly drop in long yields eases pressure on securities books. Gilts at five point three eight percent are the problem child.

Credit. I could not verify iTraxx or CDX levels this week, so I will give direction only. Credit default swaps on chipmakers and hyperscalers hit cycle wides, Oracle's five-year spread jumped to a record, and CoreWeave sits around twelve hundred. So what for banks: financial spreads have been dragged by the French sovereign and by the AI scare. Senior bank paper is better protected than equity.

Commodities. Brent settled at one hundred and nine dollars and eighty-six cents, up three point seven percent, its highest close since May, as Iranian attacks on tankers kept a supply premium in crude. Gold edged up to about four thousand one hundred and eighty-one dollars. So what for banks: oil near one hundred and ten feeds inflation, keeps central banks hiking, and hurts energy-importing sovereigns, which circles back to Paris and Rome.

Equities. The Nasdaq fell two point one percent on Friday and about four and a half percent on the week, its worst since June, on AI worries and falls in chipmakers. The S and P five hundred closed at seven thousand six hundred and fifty-nine, down one point one percent. The Dow, at fifty-two thousand and two, finished the week higher. So what for banks: trading desks like volatility, but a deeper tech sell-off hits the I P O pipeline and fees.

One view, labelled as such. My reading is that the market is repricing the cost of financing the future, in three places at once: French public debt, AI data centres and bank model risk. In each case the issue is the same. Borrowing was cheap, and some of the collateral was a promise. The banks' capital is better than in twenty twelve, as the E C B keeps reminding us. The question is who holds the second-order risks.

Market anecdotes. First, the trading boom. Goldman's equities desk made about six point four billion dollars in a single quarter, up seventy-two percent, a reminder of how much volatility is worth to the big desks. Second, a sign of the times in the AI credit market: CoreWeave's chief executive called the sell-off irrational on Wednesday, and the shares then jumped after Nvidia's chief executive said financing costs were falling. A borrower's supplier is now commenting on its cost of debt. Third, the size of the money-laundering penalty on Bank of America is a record for the Comptroller, at seven hundred and fifty million dollars, and the third-largest in Treasury history in total.

This day in market history. On the eighteenth of October, nineteen thirty-seven, American stocks fell hard. One dataset puts the drop on the S and P ninety at about nine point three percent, one of the largest single-day falls on record, during the so-called Roosevelt recession, when the government tightened too early after the recovery from the Depression. The link to today: central banks are hiking into an oil shock, with a big sovereign debt question in France. Nineteen thirty-seven is the reminder that tightening into fragility has a price. And tomorrow is the anniversary of Black Monday, the nineteenth of October nineteen eighty-seven, when the S and P five hundred fell more than twenty percent in a day. Portfolio insurance and leverage did the damage then. Today, the leverage sits in places such as private credit and data-centre debt.
